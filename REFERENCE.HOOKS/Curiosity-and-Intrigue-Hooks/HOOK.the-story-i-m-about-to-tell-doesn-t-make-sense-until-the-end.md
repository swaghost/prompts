# The story I’m about to tell doesn’t make sense—until the end

## Hook Group

Curiosity & Intrigue Hooks

## Desired Viewer Preception

The viewer perceives you as observant and insightful, someone who notices what others miss and turns it into practical clarity.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally this creates intrigue and anticipation. Intellectually it signals a hidden pattern worth understanding. Psychologically it activates curiosity gaps and closure-seeking behavior. Physically it drives scroll-stopping attention and sustained watch/read time.

## Psychology

Open loop → closure.

## Example

The story I’m about to tell about my first client doesn’t make sense—until the end.

## Why it works

This hook works because it creates an information gap the brain wants to close. By hinting that something is unusual, hidden, or unresolved, it increases attention and keeps people engaged until payoff. Specific examples make the intrigue believable and reduce perceived clickbait risk. The result is stronger retention, comments, and follow-through.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

It can fail when intrigue is too vague, too exaggerated, or not resolved clearly. Repeating the same mystery frame without meaningful payoff reduces trust. If the “twist” is weak, the audience feels manipulated. Poor specificity can make the hook feel generic and disposable.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

Overusing mystery hooks can condition audiences to expect drama over substance. If outcomes are routinely underwhelming, credibility declines quickly. Excessive suspense may attract low-intent engagement that does not convert. A persistent “secret” tone can also weaken perceived transparency over time.
