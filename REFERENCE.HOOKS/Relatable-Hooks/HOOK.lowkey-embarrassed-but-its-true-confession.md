# Lowkey embarrassed but it's true: [confession]

## Hook Group

Relatable Hooks

## Psychology

Confession triggers engagement.

## Example

Lowkey embarrassed but it's true: I still google "how to boil eggs."

## Why it works

Confession hooks work by admitting mildly embarrassing truths that create connection through vulnerability and recognition. The phrase "lowkey embarrassed" acknowledges shame without drowning in it—it's mild embarrassment, not deep shame, making the confession feel safe to share and receive. The addition "but it's true" doubles down on authenticity, emphasizing that despite embarrassment, the creator is being honest rather than manufacturing relatability. This vulnerability builds trust because the creator risks judgment to be authentic. The format also invites reciprocal confessions from viewers, creating engagement as people admit their own similar embarrassments in comments. The example about googling "how to boil eggs" is perfect because it's a task considered so basic that needing instructions feels mildly shameful, yet many people look up basic cooking tasks they feel they "should" know by their age. The confession captures the gap between assumed competence (adults should know how to boil eggs) and reality (there are still questions: starting cold or hot water? How long exactly? How to peel them easily?). The admission is both humorous and humanizing. The hook works because it normalizes not knowing "basic" things that everyone is assumed to know, providing relief and permission to admit knowledge gaps without shame. It builds community around shared gaps in basic life skills that people often hide, creating bonding through collective admission that adulting is harder than it looks and we're all still learning things others assume we already know. The confession format creates high engagement as viewers share their own "should know but don't" admissions.
