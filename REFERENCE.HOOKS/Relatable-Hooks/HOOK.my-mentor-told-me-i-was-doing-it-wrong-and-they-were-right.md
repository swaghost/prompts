# My mentor told me I was doing it wrong—and they were right

## Hook Group

Relatable Hooks

## Psychology

Authority correction (people trust "I learned the hard way" stories).

## Example

My coach told me my content strategy was a waste of time—and they were right.

## Why it works

Authority correction hooks work by demonstrating humility and the willingness to receive critical feedback, which builds credibility and trust. The admission that "they were right" signals self-awareness and lack of ego—the creator can admit being wrong, which makes them more trustworthy than someone who only claims to have always known the right answer. The reference to a "mentor" or "coach" adds a layer of authority to the criticism, suggesting it came from someone with expertise whose judgment should be respected. This positions the content as wisdom passed down from a more experienced source, not just personal opinion. The hook creates curiosity about what specifically was wrong and what the correct approach is, driving engagement to learn the corrected version. The structure also makes the viewer wonder if they might be making the same mistake, creating personal relevance. The example about content strategy being a waste of time is particularly powerful because it's harsh criticism of something the person was presumably investing significant time and effort into. Admitting this publicly demonstrates vulnerability and the willingness to pivot dramatically when evidence shows you're on the wrong path. The hook works because it tells a story of growth through correction—the creator was doing something incorrectly, received expert feedback, had the humility to accept it, and now has superior knowledge to share. This positions the content as battle-tested, ego-free wisdom rather than untested theory, which is exactly what audiences seek when trying to avoid common mistakes.
