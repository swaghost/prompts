# My coach told me my [effort] sucked

## Hook Group

Relatable Hooks

## Psychology

Harsh truth framing (pain → growth credibility).

## Example

My coach told me my sales calls sucked—and they were right.

## Why it works

Harsh-truth hooks work by sharing blunt criticism that demonstrates humility and creates curiosity about what was wrong and how to fix it. The word "sucked" is deliberately harsh and informal—it's not softened with diplomatic language like "could be improved" but uses the direct, even brutal language the coach presumably used. This rawness makes the story feel authentic rather than sanitized. The harsh criticism also creates empathy because most viewers have received difficult feedback at some point and remember how it felt, creating connection through shared vulnerability. The hook creates strong curiosity: what specifically sucked? What was the creator doing wrong? How did they fix it? What do successful sales calls look like? The reference to a "coach" adds authority to the criticism—this isn't just random negativity but expert assessment from someone qualified to evaluate performance. The phrase "my [effort] sucked" acknowledges the creator was trying and investing effort, which wasn't questioned—the issue was the approach or execution was wrong despite good intentions. The example about sales calls is particularly effective because sales is a skill many people struggle with, and the difference between effective and ineffective sales approaches is often subtle but makes massive impact on conversion rates. The hook works because it promises to reveal the gap between amateur and expert-level execution in a specific skill area, sharing lessons learned through difficult feedback. It positions growth through painful correction as credible wisdom worth sharing, building trust through authentic admission of past failures.
