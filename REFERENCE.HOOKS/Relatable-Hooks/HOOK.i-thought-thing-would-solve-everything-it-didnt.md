# I thought [thing] would solve everything. It didn't

## Hook Group

Relatable Hooks

## Psychology

Disconfirmation (expectation gap builds trust).

## Example

I thought buying a $2k course would solve everything—it didn't.

## Why it works

Disconfirmation hooks work by sharing the gap between expectations and reality, which builds trust through honesty about disappointments or unmet promises. The phrase "I thought [thing] would solve everything" establishes inflated expectations many people have about silver-bullet solutions—the belief that one purchase, one strategy, or one change will fix all problems at once. This is a common cognitive distortion, especially in marketing-saturated environments where solutions are oversold. The stark admission "it didn't" creates honesty and relatability by admitting the thing didn't live up to hopes. This vulnerability builds credibility because the creator isn't selling fantasies but sharing real experience, including disappointments. The structure also creates curiosity about what happened instead—if that didn't solve everything, what did work? What was missing? The example about buying an expensive course is particularly resonant because online courses are often marketed as complete solutions that will transform your business or life, creating unrealistic expectations. Many people buy courses hoping they contain the missing piece that will make everything click, then feel disappointed or like failures when the course alone doesn't produce results. By admitting this openly, the creator validates that experience while likely preparing to share what actually did work (probably implementation, consistency, or additional context the course didn't provide). The hook works because it punctures marketing hype with reality, building trust through honest assessment. It positions the creator as someone who's learned what actually produces results versus what merely sounds good, which is invaluable for audiences trying to avoid wasting money and time on ineffective solutions.
