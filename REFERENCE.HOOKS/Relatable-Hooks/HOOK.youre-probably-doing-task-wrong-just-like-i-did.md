# You're probably doing [task] wrong—just like I did

## Hook Group

Relatable Hooks

## Psychology

Shared mistake authority (audience learns from your error).

## Example

You're probably doing Instagram captions wrong—just like I did.

## Why it works

Shared-mistake hooks work by identifying common errors while demonstrating the creator once made the same mistakes, creating both authority (they learned the right way) and relatability (they understand the wrong approach personally). The phrase "you're probably doing [task] wrong" creates immediate self-assessment and curiosity—am I doing it wrong? What's the right way? The word "probably" softens what could be confrontational by suggesting this is a common error, not a personal failing. The crucial addition "just like I did" transforms potential defensiveness into receptivity by establishing the creator as a reformed practitioner, not a superior judge. This admission builds trust through vulnerability and positions the lesson as hard-won personal experience rather than theoretical knowledge. The structure implicitly promises before-and-after insight: here's what I was doing wrong, here's what right looks like, and here's how to transition. The example about Instagram captions is particularly effective because captions are something every Instagram user writes but few understand strategically. Common mistakes include making captions about themselves rather than the audience, failing to include calls-to-action, or writing lengthy paragraphs that don't suit the platform. The hook creates urgency to learn the right approach because if captions are wrong, every post is less effective than it could be—fixing this one element improves all content. The hook works because it combines correction with empathy, making viewers receptive to learning while validating that the mistake is understandable. It positions improvement as a simple shift rather than fundamental inadequacy, which is motivating rather than discouraging.
