# Are You Making This Mistake With Your [Thing]?

## Hook Group

Question Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should see you as a helpful guide alerting them to a common error they might be unknowingly making. You're positioned as protective and diagnostic, looking out for their success.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

- **Intellectual:** Immediate self-assessment, mistake identification attempt, curiosity about the error
- **Emotional:** Mild anxiety, curiosity, relief if discovered early, urgency if applicable
- **Psychological:** Self-audit activation, defensive checking, error-correction readiness
- **Physical:** Attention capture, mental inventory of actions, lean-in for answer

## Psychology

Direct questions demand mental response - viewers automatically check themselves against the question. The singular "this mistake" implies one specific, identifiable error rather than vague problems. Asking "are you" creates personal relevance and urgency - this isn't about others, it's about them. The question format creates an information gap that's psychologically uncomfortable until filled. By framing it as a mistake with "thing," you make it specific to their domain while maintaining curiosity about what exactly the mistake is.

## Example

"Are you making this mistake with your hooks? 95% of creators bury the hook in the second sentence instead of leading with it. If your first words aren't scroll-stopping, nothing else matters."

## Why it works

This hook works because it triggers immediate self-examination without being accusatory. The question format feels collaborative rather than judgmental - you're helping them discover something rather than criticizing. By suggesting a specific mistake exists, you create urgency to find out what it is and whether they're guilty of it. The personal address ("are you") makes it impossible to passively consume - viewers must actively assess. It positions you as helpful diagnostician rather than critic. The hook also leverages FOMO: viewers fear missing important error identification that could improve results.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails if the mistake you reveal is too obvious or too obscure - either disappointing expectations or seeming irrelevant. It can backfire if phrased too accusatorially, making viewers defensive rather than receptive. If viewers check and aren't making the mistake, they may disengage feeling the content doesn't apply. The question can feel manipulative if it's just a curiosity device without genuinely helpful insight. It also fails if you don't quickly answer - prolonging the question creates frustration rather than engagement.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can create excessive self-doubt where viewers constantly question whether they're making mistakes. It may attract an overly cautious audience that's more focused on avoiding errors than creating boldly. The questioning approach can position you as a constant fault-finder rather than a solutions provider. If overused, it can make your content feel like a series of "gotchas" rather than genuine education. The hook might also create defensive reactions where viewers rationalize their approach rather than openly considering whether it needs adjustment.
