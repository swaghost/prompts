# Are you making this mistake with your [THING]? Save this before you forget it.

## Hook Group

Question Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as diagnostically helpful—identifying specific errors viewers might not recognize in their own work. Positions the creator as protective guide preventing avoidable failure.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations

**Emotional:** Anxiety about potential unrecognized error mixed with curiosity about what the mistake might be. Creates urgency around self-assessment.

**Intellectual:** Activates **self-diagnostic thinking** and **pattern matching**. Triggers retrospective analysis of recent work against common errors.

**Psychological:** Creates **uncertainty about correctness** that can only be resolved by engaging with content. Triggers **error-checking mode** and **loss aversion**.

**Physical:** Attention spike, possible anxiety manifesting as physical tension, mental inventory of recent work.

## Psychology

This hook leverages **diagnostic uncertainty** and **error aversion** to create engagement through self-assessment motivation. The question "are you making this mistake?" creates **personal relevance** while the specificity "with your [THING]" narrows focus to particular domain.

The template works through **blindspot anxiety**. The implication is that a mistake exists that viewers might not recognize in their own work—if they knew about it, they'd have fixed it already. This creates urgency around accessing external diagnostic perspective.

"Are you making" uses present continuous tense, suggesting the mistake might be happening right now in ongoing work. This **temporal immediacy** creates urgency that past-focused ("did you make") or future-focused ("will you make") framing wouldn't generate.

"This mistake" implies **singularity and specificity**. It's not vague "some mistakes"—it's a particular identifiable error common enough to warrant dedicated attention. This focus creates anticipation while reducing cognitive load (one thing to check, not overwhelming list).

The question format is psychologically powerful because it creates **uncertainty that demands resolution**. Unlike declarative "you're making this mistake" (which triggers defensiveness), the question "are you?" invites self-assessment and maintains viewer agency.

"With your [THING]" provides **domain specificity** that makes the mistake feel immediately relevant. It's not abstract error—it's error in the specific area viewer cares about and actively works on. This personal relevance dramatically increases engagement.

"Save this before you forget it" creates **ongoing vigilance framing**. It implies the mistake is easy to make even after learning about it—either because it seems like the right approach, is widely modeled, or operates below conscious awareness. The save instruction supports repeated error-checking, not just one-time correction.

The template also activates **social proof through commonality**. If the creator is asking whether viewers make this mistake, it must be common. This reduces shame about potentially making it while amplifying the value of learning to avoid it.

For viewers currently making the mistake, the hook provides **early warning**—they can course-correct before consequences compound. For viewers not making it, the hook provides **prevention insurance** and validation that they're on solid ground.

The question also triggers **diagnostic curiosity**—viewers begin mental inventory immediately, trying to guess what the mistake might be before content reveals it. This cognitive activation prior to content consumption increases engagement depth.

There's powerful **loss aversion** activation. The focus on avoiding mistake (preventing loss) is approximately twice as motivating as pursuing optimization opportunity (pursuing gain). This negative framing leverages asymmetric human motivation.

The mistake framing also creates **clear binary assessment**—after engaging with content, viewers can definitively evaluate whether they're making the error or not, providing satisfying cognitive closure.

## Example

"Are you making this mistake with your hooks? Save this before you forget it."

This might identify common hook errors like burying the value proposition, using vague curiosity instead of specific promise, ignoring pattern interruption, or optimizing for cleverness over clarity. The question format invites self-assessment without triggering defensiveness about current practices.

## Why it works

Works because it combines diagnostic uncertainty (am I making this mistake?) with error aversion (I need to know so I can stop). The question format maintains viewer agency and reduces defensiveness compared to declarative criticism. The specificity ("this mistake" with "your [THING]") makes the content feel immediately relevant and actionable. For viewers making the mistake, it offers early warning and correction path; for viewers avoiding it, it provides validation and prevention knowledge. The "save this" element supports ongoing error-checking as persistent reference.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

Fails when the identified mistake is so obvious that viewers feel patronized, or so rare that most viewers don't make it, making the question feel misleading. If the content doesn't clearly explain what the mistake is and how to identify whether you're making it, viewers experience anxiety without resolution. Also fails if the "mistake" is actually context-dependent—beneficial in some situations, problematic in others—making blanket identification as "mistake" overly simplistic. Can trigger defensive resistance if viewers have consciously chosen the approach being labeled as mistake.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

Can backfire by creating **hypervigilance** and **self-doubt** where viewers become overly cautious about every decision, reducing creative confidence and flow. May attract viewers with **negative bias** who engage more with problem-focused content than growth-focused content. The mistake framing could also create **imposter syndrome** if viewers discover they've been making the error for extended periods, making them question their overall competence. For viewers who've been successful despite making the mistake, the hook may feel invalidating or trigger defensive rejection. The focus on single mistakes might also create **tunnel vision** where viewers fixate on avoiding this one error while missing other important improvement opportunities.
