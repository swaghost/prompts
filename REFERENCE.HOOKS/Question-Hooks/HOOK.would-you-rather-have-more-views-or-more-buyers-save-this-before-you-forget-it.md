# Would you rather have more views or more buyers? Save this before you forget it.

## Hook Group

Question Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as someone who challenges vanity metrics and refocuses attention on business fundamentals. Positions the creator as commercially sophisticated and focused on outcomes that actually matter.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations

**Emotional:** Slight defensiveness followed by reflection—recognition that current metrics pursued might not align with actual goals. Creates tension between ego satisfaction (views) and business outcomes (buyers).

**Intellectual:** Activates **values clarification** and **goal alignment assessment**. Triggers evaluation of whether effort is optimized for right outcomes.

**Psychological:** Creates **forced choice** that reveals priorities and may expose misalignment between stated goals and actual focus. Triggers **strategic reassessment**.

**Physical:** Pause in scrolling, possible defensive posture if question touches uncomfortable awareness about vanity metric focus.

## Psychology

This hook leverages **forced choice framing** and **vanity metric challenge** to create engagement through priority clarification. The binary "would you rather" format eliminates middle-ground escape and forces explicit value ranking.

The template works through **goal-behavior alignment diagnosis**. Many creators claim business goals but optimize for attention metrics. This question exposes that misalignment by forcing explicit choice between ego satisfaction (views) and commercial outcome (buyers).

"Would you rather" creates **values revelation** that declarative statements can't. Asking directly "what do you want?" gets socially acceptable answers. Forcing binary choice between two desirable things reveals actual priorities through what gets sacrificed.

"More views or more buyers" presents **fundamentally different value propositions**: views represent attention/validation/reach; buyers represent revenue/business sustainability/commercial success. For creators claiming business goals, the "correct" answer is obvious—yet many optimize for views.

The question activates **cognitive dissonance** for viewers who know the "right" answer (buyers) but recognize their actual behavior optimizes for views. This productive dissonance creates motivation to realign effort with stated priorities.

"Save this before you forget it" creates **ongoing alignment check**. It implies viewers will drift back toward vanity metrics without conscious vigilance. The save instruction supports periodic reassessment of whether effort optimizes for views (ego) or buyers (business).

The template also leverages **metric sophistication signaling**. Many creator advice emphasizes growth metrics; this hook acknowledges that not all metrics are equally valuable depending on goals. This nuance appeals to commercially serious creators.

For creators frustrated with monetization despite decent view counts, the hook provides **diagnostic clarity**—their problem isn't lack of attention but lack of attention optimization for conversion. This reframes the challenge from "get more views" to "optimize views for buyers."

There's powerful **strategic redirection** implicit in the question. Viewers who realize they've been optimizing for wrong metric can dramatically shift approach—content designed for maximum reach looks very different from content designed for buyer conversion.

The question also creates **permission to diverge from growth-at-all-costs** mentality. Many creators feel pressure to maximize views; this hook validates that smaller audiences with higher buyer conversion might be strategically superior.

The binary framing prevents **both-and avoidance**. While ideally creators want both views and buyers, the forced choice reveals which matters more when tradeoffs exist—and tradeoffs often exist between reach-optimized and conversion-optimized content.

For viewers genuinely pursuing attention-based goals (brand deals requiring reach), the question validates their view focus. For viewers pursuing product-based goals, it exposes potential misalignment.

The question also triggers **opportunity cost awareness**. Every piece of reach-optimized content represents foregone conversion-optimized content. Making this tradeoff explicit helps viewers make intentional rather than default choices.

## Example

"Would you rather have more views or more buyers? Save this before you forget it."

This might lead to content explaining that reach-optimized content (broad topics, high production, trending formats) looks different from conversion-optimized content (specific problems, authority demonstration, trust building). The question helps creators align tactics with actual business goals rather than defaulting to vanity metric pursuit.

## Why it works

Works because it forces explicit priority ranking between ego-satisfying metric (views) and business-sustaining metric (buyers), exposing potential misalignment between stated goals and actual optimization. The binary choice eliminates middle-ground avoidance and creates cognitive dissonance for viewers who know the "right" answer but recognize behavioral misalignment. For creators frustrated with monetization despite attention, it provides diagnostic clarity about optimization mismatch. The "save this" element supports ongoing priority alignment checking against vanity metric drift.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

Fails when presented to viewers whose legitimate goals are attention-based (brand deals, media attention, platform opportunities) rather than direct selling—for them, views genuinely matter more than buyers. If the content doesn't acknowledge that views and buyers aren't mutually exclusive and that optimal strategy depends on business model, it oversimplifies. Also fails if it creates false dichotomy that makes viewers feel they must choose one metric while ignoring the other entirely. Can feel judgmental toward viewers who acknowledge caring about views for non-commercial reasons (validation, impact, community).

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

Can backfire by creating **metric nihilism** where viewers dismiss all attention metrics as vanity, even when reach is genuinely important for their business model. May trigger **shame** in creators who care about views for validation rather than purely commercial reasons, making them feel their motivations are immature. The forced choice could also create **premature commercialization pressure**, making early-stage creators who should be building audience and skills feel they must focus on conversion before they're ready. For creators whose business requires scale before conversion (freemium models, community businesses), the views-to-buyers path isn't either-or but sequential. The question might also reduce **content quality** if viewers interpret "focus on buyers" as "make everything a sales pitch," damaging the trust and value that ultimately drive conversions.
