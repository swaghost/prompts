# Five Things I Would Never Do Again As A [Role]

## Hook Group

List Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should see you as someone who's been through the trial-and-error process and is willing to share hard-won lessons to save them from the same mistakes. You're positioned as experienced, humble about past errors, and genuinely helpful in sharing what you've learned.

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive you as an experienced practitioner who has made mistakes, learned from them, and is generously sharing those lessons to shortcut their learning curve. You're seen as authentic, self-aware, and trustworthy because you're willing to admit what didn't work.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

- **Intellectual:** Anticipation of specific lessons, shortcut learning, avoidance strategy formation
- **Emotional:** Gratitude for transparency, relief at avoiding pain, connection through shared struggle
- **Psychological:** Authority through experience, learning from others' mistakes, validation if they avoided these
- **Physical:** Scroll through the list, mental note-taking, compare to own actions

## Psychology

List hooks leverage several cognitive principles: they promise completeness (exactly five things, not many or some), create anticipation through structure, and allow viewers to quickly assess value. The "never again" framing is powerful because it signals strong conviction born from experience - these aren't theoretical concerns but tested failures. Humans learn powerfully from others' mistakes when those lessons are packaged as cautionary tales. The specificity of the role creates relevance and self-identification.

## Example

"Five things I would never do again as a content creator: 1) Edit for perfection before posting, 2) Ignore analytics for 'authenticity,' 3) Post without a clear CTA, 4) Chase every trend, 5) Compare my beginning to others' middle."

## Why it works

This hook works because it combines the structure and digestibility of list content with the credibility of experience-based learning. "Never again" implies these mistakes were costly enough to create permanent behavioral change, suggesting the lessons are valuable. The numbered format creates completion desire - viewers want to collect all five insights. By framing them as things you WOULD NOT do, you're implicitly telling viewers what TO do by inverse. The hook creates authority through vulnerability: admitting mistakes paradoxically builds trust more than claiming perfection. Lists also create natural share-ability and save-ability.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails if the five things are too obvious or common knowledge, disappointing viewers expecting insider insights. It can backfire if you haven't actually experienced these as a practitioner in that role, exposing you as inauthentic. If the items are too specific to your unique situation, they lack transferable value. The list format can encourage shallow treatment of each point, making the content feel superficial. It also fails if you don't explain WHY you'd never do these things again - without context, the list is just assertions without learning.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can inadvertently make your past self seem incompetent, causing viewers to question your current judgment. It may create unnecessary fear around actions that work for others but didn't work for you in specific contexts. The hook can attract an overly cautious audience focused more on avoiding mistakes than taking action. If items on your list contradict advice from other respected sources, it can create confusion rather than clarity. The "never again" absolutism can also seem rigid rather than nuanced, especially since context often matters in determining whether an approach works.
