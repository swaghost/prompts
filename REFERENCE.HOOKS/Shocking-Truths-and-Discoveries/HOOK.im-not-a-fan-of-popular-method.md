# I'm not a fan of [popular method]

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as independent-minded and willing to dissent from popular opinion. They see you as thoughtful about methods rather than blindly following trends. You appear as someone who forms opinions based on experience or analysis rather than popularity.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers curiosity about why you reject something others embrace. Intellectually activates interest in contrarian perspective on familiar method. Psychologically creates permission to question popular methods they may have doubts about. Physically manifests as stopping scroll to understand what flaws you've identified that others overlook.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because dissenting opinions on popular things create pattern interruption - you're going against social proof, which seems risky and therefore interesting. This hook leverages contrarian curiosity where audiences want to know why someone rejects what others accept. The phrase "I'm not a fan" is softer than outright condemnation, making dissent feel measured rather than extreme. By targeting specifically popular methods, you ensure audience familiarity while challenging their assumptions. The personal opinion framing ("I'm not") makes it subjective rather than absolute, reducing defensiveness.

## Example

I'm not a fan of the 10,000-hour rule: it ignores quality of practice, feedback loops, and natural aptitude differences that matter more than raw time.

## Why it works

This hook works because it validates suppressed doubts audiences may have about popular methods they've been told to adopt. By taking measured dissenting stance, you demonstrate independent thinking and analytical rigor. The hook creates intrigue about what you know that masses don't - your dissent suggests special insight. The softer framing ("not a fan" versus "this is terrible") allows audiences to consider critique without feeling attacked for using the method. This combination of doubt validation, independent thinking demonstration, insider knowledge implication, and non-threatening framing creates engaged receptive audiences.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when you can't articulate substantive criticism beyond personal preference - "not a fan" needs reasoning to be valuable. If you're contrarian about everything popular, audiences see you as reflexively oppositional rather than thoughtfully selective. The hook also fails if you strawman the popular method rather than engaging its actual strengths and trade-offs. If your alternative isn't clearly better, criticism seems like complaining without solutions. Too much dissent can isolate you from communities that do find value in popular methods.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by positioning you as perpetually critical of popular approaches, making you seem difficult or negative. You may attract audiences who enjoy criticizing popular things rather than building positive alternatives, creating toxic community. The measured dissent can seem wishy-washy compared to stronger positions, reducing your authority. If popular methods actually work well for many people, your criticism can seem ignorant of contexts where they excel. This approach can also limit partnerships with people who teach or use methods you criticize. The contrarian stance may become identity trap where you maintain opposition even when evidence suggests popular method has merit.
