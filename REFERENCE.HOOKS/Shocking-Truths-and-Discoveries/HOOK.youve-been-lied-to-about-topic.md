# You've been lied to about [topic]

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as a truth-teller who's willing to expose deception and protect them from misinformation. They see you as brave, trustworthy, and on their side against forces that mislead. You appear as someone who values their wellbeing enough to risk backlash by revealing uncomfortable truths that others won't discuss.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers anger and betrayal at being deceived, mixed with gratitude toward you for revealing truth. Intellectually activates distrust of previous information sources and heightened attention to your correction. Psychologically creates strong emotional investment through the betrayal narrative - nobody likes being lied to. Physically manifests as immediate stopping of scroll, intense focus, and often visceral reactions like jaw-clenching or forward-leaning posture.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because betrayal is one of the strongest emotional triggers humans experience. The accusation of being "lied to" activates both anger (at the deceivers) and fear (what else might be false), creating powerful motivation to discover the truth. This hook leverages betrayal triggers which create stronger attention than positive emotions because our brains prioritize threat detection. The personal framing ("you've been lied to") makes the deception feel targeted at them specifically, increasing emotional intensity and personal investment in discovering what's really true.

## Example

You've been lied to about investing: the stock market isn't as complicated as they make it seem.

## Why it works

This hook works because it transforms passive misinformation into active deception, which feels more urgent and personal. By suggesting intentional lying rather than honest mistakes, you create a villain (the liars) and position yourself as the hero correcting their wrongs. The betrayal framing removes audience shame about not knowing - they were lied to, so ignorance isn't their fault. This psychological safety combined with moral outrage creates powerful engagement. The hook also promises vindication and empowerment - learning the truth lets them avoid future manipulation and potentially enlighten others who are still deceived.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails spectacularly when you can't prove intentional deception versus innocent error or different interpretation - accusing people of lying without evidence makes you look reckless and conspiratorial. If the "lie" is actually more nuanced than you present, informed audiences will dismiss you as simplistic or clickbait. Overuse makes you seem paranoid or perpetually victimized, which repels balanced audiences. The hook also fails if your "truth" is equally questionable or partisan - replacing one bias with another doesn't establish credibility. If your tone is too aggressive, you attract angry audiences rather than curious learners, degrading community quality.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by creating a victim mentality in your audience where they see deception everywhere, including eventually in your content. The aggressive accusation can provoke legal action if you accuse specific entities of lying without sufficient evidence. You may attract conspiracy theorists and paranoid personalities who expect increasingly extreme "exposés," forcing content escalation you can't sustain. The hook positions you antagonistically toward industries or authorities, potentially blacklisting you from partnerships or opportunities. If you're proven wrong about the "lie," you suffer severe credibility damage because you made a moral accusation (lying) rather than just being factually mistaken.
