# Stop doing [common practice] - here's why

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as someone who identifies harmful or ineffective common practices. They see you as directive and confident enough to tell people to stop doing things. You appear as someone who prevents wasted effort or harm by calling out problematic norms.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers alarm that their current practice might be counterproductive. Intellectually activates critical examination of behaviors they've normalized. Psychologically creates urgency to stop potentially harmful practice immediately. Physically manifests as stopping scroll because directive "stop" command demands immediate attention to potential mistake.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because direct "stop" command creates urgency - continuing current behavior could cause harm or waste. This hook leverages loss aversion where preventing harm feels more motivating than gaining benefit. The authoritative framing demonstrates confidence in your assessment - you're not suggesting consideration but commanding cessation. By targeting "common practice," you validate why audiences do it while explaining why they should stop despite popularity. The explanation promise ("here's why") satisfies need for reasoning before abandoning established behaviors.

## Example

Stop doing daily weigh-ins - here's why: weight fluctuates 2-5 pounds daily from water, food timing, and hormones, creating anxiety and false feedback that obscures actual fat loss trends better measured weekly.

## Why it works

This hook works because it protects audiences from continuing ineffective or harmful practices, creating protective value. The directive framing cuts through analysis paralysis - you're confidently telling them what to do. By explaining why common practice is wrong, you help audiences understand not just what to change but why change matters. The hook satisfies desire for expert guidance that saves time and prevents mistakes. This combination of protective intervention, confident direction, understanding provision, and mistake prevention creates grateful action-oriented audiences.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when you command people to stop practices that actually work well in certain contexts or for certain people. If you constantly tell people to stop doing things, you seem controlling or negative rather than helpful. The hook also fails if your reasoning doesn't justify the urgency of stopping versus modifying approach. If the common practice isn't actually common, you seem disconnected from reality. Too many "stop doing" commands can create audiences paralyzed by all the things they should avoid rather than focused on positive actions.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by creating audiences oriented around avoidance rather than approach behaviors, limiting positive action. You may attract people seeking permission to quit challenges rather than guidance on effective practice. The authoritative commanding can alienate audiences who prefer collaborative discovery to directive instruction. If your "stop" advice is wrong, you've prevented people from beneficial practices with confidence, causing significant harm. This approach can also position you as perpetual critic of common practices rather than builder of better alternatives. The urgency framing may reduce nuanced understanding where practices have both costs and benefits worth considering.
