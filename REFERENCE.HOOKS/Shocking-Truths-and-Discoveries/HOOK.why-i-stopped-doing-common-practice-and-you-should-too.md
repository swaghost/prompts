# Why I stopped doing [common practice] and you should too

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as someone who questions established practices and makes changes based on discovered problems. They see you as willing to abandon common approaches when evidence suggests they're harmful. You appear as someone whose behavioral change validates concerns worth examining.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers curiosity about what problem caused you to stop established practice. Intellectually activates critical examination of practice they may currently follow. Psychologically creates permission to abandon harmful common practices. Physically manifests as stopping scroll because behavior change story promises both problem revelation and solution demonstration.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because someone stopping common practice signals discovered problem - understanding what they learned enables protective behavior change. This hook leverages social proof reversal where abandoning widespread practice requires compelling reasons worth understanding. The "should too" framing extends your experience to universal recommendation. By sharing personal change, you demonstrate conviction through action not just opinion. This creates credible urgency through demonstrated behavioral commitment.

## Example

Why I stopped checking email first thing in morning and you should too: it hands control of your attention and priorities to other people's agendas, using peak cognitive hours for reactive responding instead of proactive important work - shifted to 2-hour protected morning block, email moved to midday, transformed output quality and strategic progress.

## Why it works

This hook works because it provides both problem identification and demonstrated solution through your behavioral change. The personal-to-universal arc makes abstract advice concrete. By revealing what caused you to stop, you expose problems audiences might not recognize. The hook satisfies desire for battle-tested recommendations from someone who's made the change. This combination of credible urgency, demonstrated commitment, problem exposure, and proven solution creates action-motivated audiences.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when your stopped practice works well for many people in contexts different from yours. If you constantly stop common practices, you seem contrarian or unable to maintain habits. The hook also fails if you don't explain what you replaced practice with - stopping without alternative leaves audiences stuck. If benefits you claim from stopping are placebo or coincidental, recommendation misleads. Too many should-stop recommendations can make audiences feel their current practices are all wrong.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by creating guilt in audiences who can't or won't stop common practices for legitimate reasons. You may attract people looking for permission to abandon things requiring discipline. The constant practice-stopping can position you as someone constantly changing approaches rather than committing long-term. If your stopped practice was actually beneficial and you misattribute problems to it, you've adopted less effective alternative. This approach can also create division between people who stop versus continue practice. The universal recommendation may not account for important contextual differences that make practice valuable for others.
