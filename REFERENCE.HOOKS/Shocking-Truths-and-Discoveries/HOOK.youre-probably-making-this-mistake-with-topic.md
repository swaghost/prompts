# You're probably making this mistake with [topic]

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as someone who identifies common unnoticed errors. They see you as observant about patterns that most people miss in their own behavior. You appear as someone who can spot mistakes people don't realize they're making.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers concern mixed with curiosity about potential blind spot. Intellectually activates self-examination of their current practices. Psychologically creates defensive need to identify if they're making this mistake. Physically manifests as stopping scroll because "you're probably" makes it feel personally relevant and likely applicable to them specifically.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because "you're probably" creates high likelihood of personal relevance - this isn't about others, it's likely about you. This hook leverages blind spot recognition where people want to identify unconscious errors they can't see themselves. The "probably" softening reduces defensiveness compared to "you are making" while maintaining strong relevance. By framing as mistake rather than just improvement opportunity, you emphasize cost of continuing current approach. The universal "you're" makes each individual feel addressed while "probably" acknowledges not everyone will relate.

## Example

You're probably making this mistake with email productivity: treating your inbox as a to-do list, which means your priorities are controlled by other people's urgencies rather than your own important work.

## Why it works

This hook works because it combines high personal relevance with low defensiveness through probability framing. The mistake identification provides immediate practical value by revealing blind spots. By framing error as common ("probably"), you normalize it rather than suggesting audiences are uniquely flawed. The hook satisfies desire to optimize by identifying unconscious inefficiencies. This combination of personal relevance, defensiveness reduction, blind spot revelation, and optimization opportunity creates engaged self-examining audiences.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when your identified "mistake" is actually fine or even best practice in certain contexts. If you constantly tell audiences they're "probably making mistakes," you create anxiety rather than helpful awareness. The hook also fails if your mistake is so uncommon that most audiences don't relate, making "probably" seem inaccurate. If you can't clearly explain why it's a mistake and what to do instead, you create anxiety without solution. Too much mistake-identification can make audiences feel they're constantly wrong rather than mostly right with room for improvement.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by creating anxious audiences constantly questioning if they're making mistakes they don't see. You may attract people who focus on errors rather than leveraging strengths, creating deficit-minded community. The constant mistake-calling can position you as someone who sees flaws rather than possibilities. If your identified mistakes are actually debatable or contextual, you seem rigid about "right ways" that don't exist. This approach can also make audiences defensive - even with "probably" softening, being told you're likely wrong feels judgmental. The self-examination focus may reduce forward momentum as audiences get stuck analyzing current approaches rather than trying new ones.
