# Here's why [common belief] is a lie.

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as a myth-buster who exposes false beliefs that most people accept as truth, offering liberation from limiting or misleading conventional wisdom through evidence-based debunking.

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as a myth-buster who exposes false beliefs that most people accept as truth, offering liberation from limiting or misleading conventional wisdom through evidence-based debunking.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel:

- Shock at learning something they believed is false
- Curiosity about why the belief is wrong
- Validation if they've been skeptical of the belief
- Liberation from limiting or misleading beliefs
- Engagement with challenging mainstream assumptions
- Gratitude for being freed from falsehood

## Psychology

Calling something a "lie" is confrontational and creates strong emotional response—lies suggest intentional deception or at least significant falsehood. This triggers our desire to avoid being fooled and our need to update beliefs when shown they're false. Debunking common beliefs creates value through correction—helping people stop wasting effort on wrong assumptions. It also positions the creator as an independent thinker who questions rather than accepts conventional wisdom. The format creates curiosity through promised debunking while making viewers who accept the belief feel potentially misled, creating urgency to learn the truth.

## Example

"Here's why 'follow your passion' is a lie: passion follows mastery, not the other way around—telling people to find their passion is backwards causality."

## Why it works

This hook leverages the power of belief reversal to capture attention and create memorable content. The direct language ("is a lie") creates controversy and strong positioning rather than hedged ambiguity. It offers value through correction, helping viewers avoid continuing to believe and act on false information. The format also creates differentiation—the creator thinks differently from the mainstream, suggesting independent analysis and truth-seeking. It appeals to people's desire to be "in the know" rather than fooled by commonly accepted falsehoods. The promise of explanation ("here's why") suggests evidence-based debunking rather than mere assertion, building credibility.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails when the belief challenged isn't actually false but rather context-dependent, oversimplifying nuanced topics into binary true/false framing. It loses credibility if the "lie" is just the creator's opinion or if the explanation relies on weak evidence or logic. If the common belief actually has merit and the creator's debunking misses important context, it can make the creator appear wrong and damage credibility. The hook also underperforms when the belief isn't actually commonly held, making the debunking feel like attacking a strawman. When the language is unnecessarily aggressive or dismissive toward people who believe it, it alienates rather than educates.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This hook can make the creator appear contrarian for contrarianism's sake, always finding problems with mainstream beliefs rather than sometimes validating them. It may attract a cynical audience that defines itself by rejecting common wisdom, creating community around negativity rather than shared positive values. The "lie" framing can feel aggressive or disrespectful toward people who hold the belief, creating defensive reactions rather than open learning. If the creator is later shown to be wrong, or if the "lie" is actually true in contexts they didn't consider, it severely damages credibility and judgment. The format can also contribute to broader social distrust and conspiracy thinking by suggesting common beliefs are frequently false or deceptive.
