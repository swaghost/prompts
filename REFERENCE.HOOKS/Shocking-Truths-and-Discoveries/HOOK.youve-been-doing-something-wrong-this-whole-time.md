# You've been [doing something] wrong this whole time.

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as someone who can identify widespread errors in common practices and offer correct methods, positioning them as an expert who sees what most people miss in everyday activities.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel:

- Surprise at learning they've been doing something incorrectly
- Curiosity about the right way to do it
- Relief that struggles weren't their fault but method issues
- Eagerness to learn the correct approach
- Validation if they've felt the current method was inefficient
- Mild embarrassment offset by gratitude for correction

## Psychology

This hook triggers the information gap response—learning we've been doing something wrong creates urgent desire to know the correct method. It also provides relief by externalizing blame from personal inadequacy to incorrect method. The "this whole time" element adds weight by suggesting prolonged error, making correction more valuable. This taps into our desire for optimization and efficiency—if we've been doing something inefficiently, learning the right way immediately improves results. It positions the creator as having specialized knowledge that allows them to spot errors others don't notice, establishing authority through corrective expertise.

## Example

"You've been setting goals wrong this whole time: outcome goals create anxiety—process goals create progress. Stop focusing on hitting 100K, start focusing on doing the daily work."

## Why it works

This hook creates immediate personal relevance by addressing common actions viewers likely perform, making the content directly applicable. The "wrong this whole time" framing creates mild discomfort that demands resolution, driving engagement. It promises immediate improvement—once you know the right way, results should improve. The format also humanizes error by suggesting it's widespread rather than personal failure, reducing defensiveness. It establishes the creator as detail-oriented expert who can identify inefficiencies in standard practices. The implicit promise of optimization (right way is better than wrong way) creates strong motivation to learn and implement.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails when the "wrong" way actually works fine for many people, making the correction feel needlessly critical or like claiming false superiority. It loses credibility if the "right" way is just the creator's preference, context-dependent, or not actually better for all situations. If viewers feel attacked or stupid for doing it "wrong," it creates defensiveness rather than openness to learning. The hook also underperforms when the difference between wrong and right way is trivial or nitpicky, not actually affecting outcomes. When there isn't actually widespread wrong practice but rather diverse valid approaches, the hook feels false.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This hook can make viewers feel incompetent or stupid, creating shame rather than empowerment if not framed carefully. It may attract perfectionists or people with anxiety who then feel they're doing everything wrong, never good enough. The format can become a crutch where the creator always claims people are doing things wrong, making them appear perpetually critical rather than constructive. If the "right" way doesn't work better for many viewers, it damages trust and makes the creator seem out of touch. The hook can also create unnecessary self-doubt in people whose methods actually work fine for their contexts, undermining confidence without justification.
