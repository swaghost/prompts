# Turns out everything we knew about [topic] was wrong

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as someone who stays current with paradigm-shifting research or discoveries. They see you as intellectually humble, willing to update beliefs when evidence changes. You appear as someone tracking collective knowledge evolution and helping audiences stay current.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers excitement about paradigm shift mixed with concern about operating on outdated information. Intellectually activates curiosity about what new understanding replaces old beliefs. Psychologically creates openness to fundamental belief revision - if everything we knew is wrong, major update is needed. Physically manifests as stopping scroll because comprehensive wrongness suggests they're operating on dangerously outdated mental models.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because "everything was wrong" signals total paradigm shift requiring complete knowledge reconstruction - partial updates aren't sufficient. This hook leverages the appeal of being on cutting edge of knowledge, getting paradigm-shifting information before it's mainstream. The collective framing ("we knew") includes you in the previously mistaken group, reducing defensiveness through shared error rather than finger-pointing. The past tense ("was wrong") with present acknowledgment ("turns out") creates narrative of intellectual progress audiences can join.

## Example

Turns out everything we knew about fat and heart disease was wrong: recent meta-analyses show saturated fat intake has no significant association with cardiovascular disease - the sugar industry funded research that blamed fat instead.

## Why it works

This hook works because it offers audiences chance to update their knowledge foundation before others, creating intellectual advantage. By admitting you also believed wrong information, you model intellectual humility and growth. The totality framing ("everything") justifies audience time investment - this isn't minor correction but major revision. The hook satisfies desire to be "in the know" about latest understanding rather than operating on outdated paradigms. This combination of early-adopter advantage, humility modeling, investment justification, and currency satisfaction creates engaged knowledge-seeking audiences.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when new information doesn't actually invalidate everything previously known, just nuances or updates it, making totality claim dishonest. If you constantly claim everything we knew is wrong, audiences realize you're exaggerating for effect. The hook also fails if you replace old "wrong" information with equally uncertain new information presented as absolute truth. If you can't clearly explain what was wrong and why new understanding is better, paradigm shift claim feels unsupported. Too many total reversals can make audiences feel knowledge is unreliable rather than progressive.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by creating audiences who distrust all established knowledge, assuming everything will eventually be proven wrong. You may attract audiences who love paradigm shifts more than accurate information, creating community that values novelty over truth. The constant revolution framing can position you as unreliable - if you keep claiming old information was wrong, why trust current information? If your "new" information is actually controversial or poorly supported, you mislead audiences who trust your paradigm-shift framing. This approach can also alienate experts in fields where you claim everything is wrong, limiting your credibility. The totality emphasis may reduce nuance recognition where old and new understanding both have partial validity.
