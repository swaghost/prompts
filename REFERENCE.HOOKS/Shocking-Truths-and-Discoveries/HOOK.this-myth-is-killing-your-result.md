# This myth is killing your [result]

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as someone who identifies and destroys harmful misconceptions that are actively sabotaging their progress. They see you as a myth-buster who provides clarity and correction, positioning you as knowledgeable, protective, and results-oriented. You appear as someone who cares enough to warn them about dangerous beliefs before more damage is done.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers fear mixed with hope - fear that they've been following bad advice, hope that you'll provide the correction. Intellectually activates critical thinking about their current beliefs and practices. Psychologically creates urgency to discover what myth is hurting them and how to fix it before more damage occurs. Physically manifests as immediate attention, stopping scroll, and leaning in to identify the specific myth and its solution.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because this hook combines loss aversion (something is "killing" their results) with myth-busting authority. Humans have a powerful negative bias - we pay more attention to threats than opportunities. The word "killing" intensifies urgency and danger, making the revelation feel life-or-death important. Myth destruction feels liberating because it promises to remove an invisible obstacle, creating the sensation of "unlocking freedom" from false constraints. The hook suggests the audience has been unknowingly sabotaging themselves, which triggers both self-protective attention and desire for redemption.

## Example

This myth is killing your Instagram reach: posting more doesn't equal more engagement.

## Why it works

This hook works because it reframes failure as not being the audience's fault but the fault of bad information they trusted. This removes shame while creating hope that simple correction will unlock results. The "killing" language creates vivid urgency that cuts through noise and forces engagement. By positioning a myth as the villain rather than the person, you create psychological safety while still delivering hard truth. The hook also promises immediate value - identifying and correcting one myth can theoretically solve their entire problem, making it feel like a high-leverage insight worth immediate attention.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when the "myth" you're busting isn't actually widely believed, making your correction feel like arguing against a strawman. If you can't provide clear evidence that this myth causes harm, your claim feels exaggerated and loses credibility. Overuse makes you seem like a contrarian who just disagrees with everything for attention rather than offering genuine insight. The hook also fails if your alternative to the myth is equally unproven or impractical - you must replace the bad belief with demonstrably better guidance. If your tone is too alarmist without proportional danger, audiences feel manipulated by fear-mongering.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by making your audience paranoid about all their beliefs, creating analysis paralysis where they distrust everything including your advice. If the myth you attack is something they're emotionally invested in, they may defensively reject you rather than the myth. The hook can position you as a constant critic, attracting negative personality types who only engage with debunking content rather than building content. If you're wrong about something being a myth, or if the "myth" actually works for many people, you lose credibility and appear dogmatic. This approach can also alienate colleagues or mentors who taught or advocate for the "myth," damaging professional relationships unnecessarily.
