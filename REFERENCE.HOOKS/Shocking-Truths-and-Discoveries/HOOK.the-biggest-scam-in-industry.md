# The biggest scam in [industry]

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as a protective whistleblower who exposes fraudulent practices to safeguard their interests. They see you as courageous, principled, and on their side against exploitative forces. You appear as someone with insider knowledge who values ethics over industry loyalty.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers anger at being exploited mixed with gratitude toward you for the warning. Intellectually activates threat assessment and critical evaluation of industry practices. Psychologically creates defensive learning - attention peaks because ignoring this could mean continued exploitation. Physically manifests as immediate scroll-stopping, forward posture, and heightened alertness to identify and avoid the scam.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because the word "scam" signals intentional exploitation, which triggers powerful threat detection systems. Humans prioritize learning about threats more than opportunities because avoiding harm was more evolutionarily critical than gaining benefits. This hook exposes threat through the defensive learning mechanism - audiences pay rapt attention to information that protects them from harm. The superlative "biggest" intensifies urgency by suggesting this is the most important scam to know about, creating fear of missing critical protective information.

## Example

The biggest scam in online coaching: selling "proprietary methods" that are just repackaged free information.

## Why it works

This hook works because it positions you as a defender of the audience against predatory actors, creating immediate trust and alliance. The exposé framing makes your content feel investigative and valuable - you're providing a public service by revealing exploitation. By calling something a "scam," you create clear villains (the scammers) and victims (your audience), which simplifies moral framing and makes sharing feel like a civic duty. The hook also triggers loss aversion - people will invest more attention to avoid losing money or being exploited than to gain equivalent benefits. This protective framing makes your content feel essential rather than optional.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails catastrophically if you can't prove intentional fraud versus aggressive marketing or questionable practices - falsely accusing businesses of scamming can trigger legal action. If your "scam" is actually a legitimate business model you personally dislike, you lose credibility with informed audiences. Overuse makes you seem like a conspiracy theorist who sees scams everywhere, which repels balanced viewers. The hook also fails if you benefit from exposing the "scam" in ways that create conflict of interest - audiences may see you as attacking competitors rather than protecting consumers. If your tone is too alarmist without proportional danger, it feels like fear-mongering.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by inviting defamation lawsuits if your scam accusations target specific companies without sufficient evidence. You may attract an angry, paranoid audience that expects increasingly extreme exposés, forcing unsustainable content escalation. The aggressive framing can burn bridges throughout your industry, isolating you professionally and limiting future opportunities. If you're proven wrong about something being a scam, you suffer severe credibility loss because you made a moral accusation. This approach can also create a culture of cynicism where audiences trust nothing, including legitimate offerings in your space, harming the entire ecosystem.
