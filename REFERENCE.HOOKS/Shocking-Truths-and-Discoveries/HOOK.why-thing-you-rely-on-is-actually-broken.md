# Why [thing you rely on] is actually broken

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as someone who identifies fundamental flaws in systems or tools they depend on. They see you as analytically sharp and willing to challenge comfortable assumptions about reliable things. You appear as a critical thinker who helps them recognize when familiar solutions are actually problems.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers concern about something they depend on daily, mixed with curiosity about what's wrong. Intellectually activates reassessment of trusted systems or tools they rarely question. Psychologically challenges comfort zone - we prefer believing our relied-upon things work well, so contradiction demands attention. Physically manifests as stopping scroll, heightened alertness, and defensive interest to evaluate whether their reliance is misplaced.

## Psychology

The mind interrupts to pay attention because this hook attacks comfort zones and dependency blindness. Humans develop psychological attachment to things we rely on regularly, making it difficult to see their flaws objectively. By explicitly stating that something relied-upon is "broken," you force cognitive dissonance between their trust and your critique. The word "actually" suggests hidden dysfunction beneath surface functionality, triggering pattern-seeking behavior to identify what they've been missing. This hook leverages threat detection - if something you depend on is broken, continuing to rely on it courts danger or waste.

## Example

Why your morning routine is actually broken: it's built for someone else's energy cycle, not yours.

## Why it works

This hook works because it reframes frustration or suboptimal results as not being the user's fault but the system's fault, which is psychologically liberating. By challenging reliance, you position yourself as someone who thinks independently rather than accepting inherited wisdom. The hook validates suspicions audiences may have had but dismissed - many people sense something isn't quite working but blame themselves. Your confirmation that the thing itself is broken removes self-blame and creates alliance. This combination of validation, liberation, and independent thinking creates strong trust and engagement.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when you attack things that actually work well for most people, making you seem contrarian or out of touch. If you can't provide clear evidence of how something is "broken," your critique feels like baseless negativity. Overuse makes you appear relentlessly critical of everything, which exhausts audiences who need constructive solutions. The hook also fails if you don't offer viable alternatives - identifying brokenness without solutions creates frustration rather than empowerment. If your "this is broken" claim is too extreme or one-sided, balanced audiences dismiss you as lacking nuance.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by making audiences paranoid about all their dependencies, creating analysis paralysis where they don't trust any systems or tools. You may develop a reputation as someone who only tears down rather than builds up, limiting audience growth to cynics. If audiences have emotional investment in what you call broken (e.g., their morning routine), they may defensively reject you rather than the system. The constant challenge of comfort zones can make your content feel destabilizing rather than improving. If you're proven wrong about something being broken, or if it works perfectly for some people, you lose credibility and appear dogmatic.
