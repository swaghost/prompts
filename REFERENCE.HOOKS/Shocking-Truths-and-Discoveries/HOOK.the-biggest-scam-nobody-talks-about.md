# The biggest scam nobody talks about

## Hook Group

Shocking Truths & Discoveries

## Desired Viewer Preception

Viewer perceives you as investigative voice uncovering exploitative practices others ignore. They see you as protective of audience interests. You appear as someone whose scam-revelation demonstrates you research beyond surface level protecting people from widespread exploitation.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Emotionally triggers protective anger about exploitation combined with fear of being victimized. Intellectually activates critical skepticism toward common practices. Psychologically creates trust through warning provision. Physically manifests as stopping scroll because "scam nobody talks about" promises protection from widespread but unspoken exploitation.

## Example

The biggest scam nobody talks about in online courses: selling "community" as value when it's just Facebook group with zero moderation - most course creators charge $2K promising community but deliver abandoned group. Students pay for connection; receive empty promise. Scam works because checking before buying is hard. Widespread but unmentioned. Empty community masking as premium feature.

## Why it works

This hook works because it combines scandal revelation with protective framing positioning you as guardian against exploitation. The "nobody talks about" element suggests you're breaking silence on widespread problem. By exposing scam mechanics, you demonstrate investigative depth. The hook satisfies desire for protection from manipulation. This combination of scandal exposure, protective positioning, silence-breaking, and exploitation revelation creates audiences who trust your judgment and appreciate your watchdog role.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

This hook fails when you label standard business practices as "scams" for dramatic effect or when you manufacture outrage about debatable issues. If you constantly expose scams, you foster paranoia and excessive distrust of all commerce. The hook also fails if you use scam-calling to position only your offerings as ethical. If practice isn't actually deceptive, dramatic framing is dishonest. Too many scam revelations can create audience paralysis through overwhelming fear of exploitation.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This can backfire by creating audiences who see scams everywhere becoming unable to make purchases or trust anyone. You may attract people who bond through outrage and victimhood narratives. The constant scam-exposure focus can reduce appreciation for legitimate business models and ethical practitioners. If your "scam" is actually poor execution not intentional deception, you've mislabeled and harmed legitimate businesses. This approach can also foster belief commerce is inherently exploitative. The scandal emphasis may reduce understanding that while some practices are deceptive, most disappointing experiences reflect poor execution rather than malicious intent, and calling everything a scam often reflects inability to distinguish intentional fraud from disappointing quality.
