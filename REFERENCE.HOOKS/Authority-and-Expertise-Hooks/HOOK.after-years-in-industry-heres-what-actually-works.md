# After [Years] Years in [Industry], Here's What Actually Works

## Hook Group

Authority Hooks, Experience-Based Hooks, Contrarian Wisdom Hooks, Time-Tested Advice Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

Viewers should perceive this as hard-won wisdom from someone who has survived long enough to separate signal from noise. The creator should feel like a trusted veteran cutting through industry BS to share what genuinely matters.

## Desired Viewer Perception (continued)

The viewer should sense that what follows will contradict popular advice or simplify complex landscapes based on real-world experience, not theory.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

**Emotional:** Respect for experience, relief at getting curated advice, anticipation of insider knowledge, gratitude for shortcut to wisdom

**Intellectual:** Heightened attention to capture distilled lessons, readiness to compare advice against what they've heard before

**Psychological:** Trust in authority, desire to skip painful learning curves, hope that expertise can be transferred, curiosity about what's been filtered out

**Physical:** Note-taking posture, screenshot preparation, full attention commitment, potential pause for deeper processing

## Psychology

This hook leverages multiple psychological principles: authority bias (we trust experienced sources), survivorship credibility (lasting years in an industry signals competence), and the appeal of curated knowledge. The phrase "what actually works" creates a powerful frame—it implies the existence of things that don't work, positioning the speaker as someone who has tested extensively and can save viewers wasted effort. The time investment mentioned (years) serves as social proof of commitment and suggests the insights have been refined through repeated real-world application. The hook also taps into our cognitive bias toward shortcuts—we'd rather learn from others' mistakes than make them ourselves. The "actually works" phrasing also subtly positions competing advice as theoretical or unproven, making this content feel more practical and reliable.

## Example

"After 12 years in copywriting, here's what actually works: Write for one person. Make one promise. Prove it. That's it."

"After 8 years running an agency, here's what actually works: Charge 3x what you think you can, deliver 2x what they expect, and fire bad clients fast."

"After 15 years teaching online, here's what actually works: Transformation beats information. Accountability beats motivation. Systems beat inspiration."

## Why it works

This hook works because it offers pattern-recognition from extensive experience in compressed, actionable form. Viewers get the benefit of years of trial-and-error without the time investment or risk. The credibility comes not just from the years mentioned but from the confidence of the claim—"here's what actually works" suggests the creator has eliminated variables, tested alternatives, and arrived at verified conclusions. The hook also creates instant differentiation from generic advice-givers; anyone can share tips, but only those with sustained experience can credibly say "this is what survives real-world contact." The format invites bullet-point clarity, making the content feel accessible and implementable. Additionally, the hook appeals to both beginners (who want expert shortcuts) and intermediate practitioners (who want validation or correction of their approaches).

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails if the creator can't back up the claim with specific, nuanced insights that clearly come from experience. If the advice that follows is generic ("work hard," "be consistent"), viewers feel misled—they expected insider knowledge, not platitudes. The hook also doesn't work if the creator hasn't actually been in the industry for the stated years or if their results don't reflect success (claiming 10 years of experience but demonstrating beginner-level understanding destroys trust). Another failure mode: if what follows is too dense, academic, or jargon-heavy, the "here's what actually works" promise feels violated—viewers expected simplification, not complexity. The hook can also fall flat if industry-specific and used with audiences outside that industry who can't assess the credibility or relevance of the claims.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This hook can backfire by triggering skepticism rather than trust, especially with experienced viewers who may think "Just because it worked for you doesn't mean it's universal." If your advice contradicts other respected authorities, you may spark debates that undermine rather than establish credibility. The time-based authority can also age poorly—if you say "after 5 years" in a rapidly changing industry, that might not be enough to impress; if you say "after 30 years," younger audiences might see you as out of touch. The hook can also create pressure to oversimplify; in trying to deliver clear, "this is what works" advice, you may strip away important nuance or context that determines whether the advice actually applies. Finally, if your audience tries your "what actually works" advice and doesn't see results (often due to different contexts or execution), they'll blame you for misleading them, damaging your reputation.
