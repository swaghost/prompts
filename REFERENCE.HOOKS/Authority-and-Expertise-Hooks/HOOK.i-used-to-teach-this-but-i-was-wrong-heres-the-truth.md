# I used to teach this — but I was wrong. Here's the truth.

## Hook Group

Authority & Expertise Hooks

## Desired Viewer Preception

The creator has evolved in their thinking and is humble enough to admit past errors, making them more trustworthy than those who never acknowledge mistakes. They're offering updated, corrected wisdom that supersedes their own previous teaching.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel they're getting the most current and honest perspective from someone who values truth over ego. Intellectually, they're curious about what changed the creator's mind and what the new truth is. Psychologically, the vulnerability and admission of being wrong creates strong trust and credibility. There's also curiosity about whether viewers themselves have been following outdated advice.

## Psychology

Vulnerability + credibility — expert who evolves is more trusted.

## Example

I used to preach daily posting. I was wrong. Here's the better way.

## Why it works

This hook is counterintuitively powerful because admitting error actually increases rather than decreases credibility. It demonstrates intellectual honesty, humility, and a commitment to truth over ego. Viewers trust experts who can admit mistakes more than those who never acknowledge being wrong, because it shows they're continuously learning and refining their understanding. The hook creates intrigue about what caused the creator to change their mind—new data, experience, or insight. It positions the creator as evolving and current rather than stuck in outdated thinking. The vulnerability makes the creator more relatable and human. The hook also serves as a correction mechanism for viewers who may have followed the previous advice, making them lean in to learn the updated perspective.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

If the creator changes their position too frequently, the pattern of "I was wrong" becomes unreliable rather than humble, making viewers wonder if the new position will also be revised soon. The previous position and new position must both be clearly articulated so viewers understand what changed and why. If the creator doesn't explain what evidence or experience changed their mind, the reversal seems arbitrary. The hook can fail if it's used manipulatively to disavow previous content without genuine evolution in thinking. If the "truth" revealed is controversial or conflicts with widely accepted practices, the creator must provide strong reasoning to avoid seeming contrarian for attention.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

Admitting you were wrong can open you to criticism from people who followed your previous advice and feel misled or harmed by it. It may reduce confidence in your current teachings—if you were wrong before, how do viewers know you're right now? The hook can attract cynics who will constantly question your authority and wait for the next reversal. If the previous teaching is still working for some people, they may feel invalidated or confused by the new position. The vulnerability might be perceived as instability or lack of expertise rather than growth. If competitors use the admission to attack your credibility broadly (not just on that specific topic), it can have ripple effects. The hook also requires the creator to have an established teaching history, so newer creators cannot authentically use it.
