# I've coached 1,000+ people and here's what most get wrong

## Hook Group

Authority & Expertise Hooks

## Desired Viewer Preception

The creator has extensive coaching experience and has identified the most common mistake or misconception that holds people back. They have a bird's-eye view of where people typically go wrong and can help viewers avoid those pitfalls.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel both concerned (am I making this mistake?) and curious to learn what the common error is. Intellectually, they're interested in pattern-based wisdom that comes from seeing many cases. Psychologically, there's a fear-of-missing-out and self-assessment impulse—people want to know if they're in the majority who get it wrong. The large number (1,000+) creates strong social proof and authority.

Social proof + expert "insider" perspective.

## Example

I've coached 1,000+ creators — most still miss this basic hook strategy.

## Why it works

This hook combines volume-based authority with the psychological draw of common mistakes. The claim of coaching "1,000+ people" establishes credibility through sheer experience and pattern recognition across diverse cases. The phrase "what most get wrong" triggers self-assessment and curiosity—viewers immediately wonder if they're making the same mistake. It also creates an implicit in-group (those who get it right) and out-group (the majority who don't), motivating viewers to learn so they can join the informed minority. The hook suggests the creator has diagnostic expertise—they can spot the error that others miss. It works particularly well because it addresses a universal fear: wasting time on the wrong approach.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

If the "mistake" revealed is too obvious or if it's something the audience already knows to avoid, the buildup feels wasted and damages credibility. The number (1,000+) must be believable given the creator's age, career length, and coaching model—if it seems inflated, everything else becomes suspect. The common mistake must be genuinely common, not a strawman that few people actually make. If the hook is overused across multiple pieces of content, audiences will tire of being told what they're doing wrong. The mistake must also be presented constructively, not in a condescending way that makes viewers feel stupid for not knowing better.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

Leading with "what most get wrong" can feel negative or critical, potentially making viewers defensive rather than receptive. If viewers have been making the mistake, they may feel called out or embarrassed, creating negative associations with the creator. The hook can attract criticism from other coaches or experts who disagree with the assessment of what constitutes a "mistake." It may also create an us-versus-them dynamic that alienates the "most people" who are getting it wrong. If the mistake is presented without adequate empathy for why people make it, the creator can seem out of touch with the real challenges their audience faces. The emphasis on volume (1,000+) might make some viewers question the quality or personalization of the coaching if the focus seems to be on quantity.
