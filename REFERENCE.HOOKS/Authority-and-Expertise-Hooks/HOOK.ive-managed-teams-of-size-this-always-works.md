# I've managed teams of [size] — this always works.

## Hook Group

Authority & Expertise Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as an experienced leader with extensive management experience across large groups, who has identified universal principles that consistently produce results regardless of team composition or context.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel:

- Confidence in scale-tested leadership principles
- Trust in someone who has managed many people
- Relief at finding universal management truths
- Respect for proven organizational experience
- Anticipation for reliable, people-tested advice
- Validation that effective leadership follows patterns

## Psychology

Leadership and management credibility comes from successfully guiding groups, especially large ones. The specific team size creates concrete proof of leadership scope and impact. The phrase "always works" suggests the creator has identified consistent patterns across diverse teams, contexts, and individuals, implying the advice transcends personality or situation. Scale matters—managing larger teams is generally seen as more challenging and credible than managing small groups. This taps into our desire for proven people-management principles that work reliably rather than approaches that only work with certain personality types or conditions.

## Example

"I've managed teams of 150+ — this always works: public praise, private criticism, always."

## Why it works

This hook establishes leadership authority through scale and repetition. The specific number of team members creates credible evidence of extensive management experience. "Always works" is a bold claim that creates strong anticipation while suggesting the principle is fundamental to human nature rather than context-dependent. The format implies the creator has tried many approaches across many people and contexts, and this one consistently delivers results. It appeals to anyone in leadership, aspiring to leadership, or trying to understand effective management. The universal claim makes it feel valuable to a broad audience while the specificity of team size makes it believable.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails when the team size mentioned is too small to be impressive or so large it becomes unbelievable. It loses credibility when the advice shared is too obvious, something anyone would know without managing large teams. If the "always works" claim is too absolute and viewers can immediately think of situations where it wouldn't apply, it undermines credibility. The hook also underperforms when it's not clear what industries, contexts, or types of teams were managed, making the experience feel vague. When the advice is too simplistic for the complex realities of managing real humans, it disappoints experienced leaders.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This hook can make the creator appear out of touch with modern, flat organizational structures or remote/distributed teams if their experience is dated or traditional. It may alienate individual contributors or small team leaders who feel the advice doesn't scale down to their context. The absolute claim of "always works" invites counterexamples and can make the creator seem rigid or unwilling to adapt to different team dynamics or cultures. If the size of teams managed is later questioned or revealed to be exaggerated, it severely damages credibility. The hook can also make management seem formulaic, ignoring the human complexity and individual attention that effective leadership requires.
