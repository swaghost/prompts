# Here's what top performers do differently from everyone else

## Hook Group

Authority & Expertise Hooks

## Desired Viewer Preception

The creator has studied or worked with elite performers and can identify the specific behaviors, mindsets, or strategies that separate them from the average. They're offering insight into the distinguishing factors of excellence.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel curious about what separates the best from the rest and motivated to adopt those differentiating behaviors. Intellectually, they're looking for specific, actionable differences they can implement. Psychologically, the comparison creates self-reflection and aspiration—viewers want to move from the "everyone else" category to the "top performer" category. There's implied hope that the difference is learnable rather than innate.

## Psychology

Comparison effect — makes audience self-reflect.

## Example

Here's what top CEOs do differently from average entrepreneurs.

## Why it works

This hook leverages the power of contrast and comparison to create clarity about what excellence requires. By explicitly comparing "top performers" to "everyone else," it highlights that success isn't random but based on specific, differentiating choices. The comparison triggers self-assessment—viewers automatically place themselves somewhere on that spectrum and want to understand what they need to change. The hook implies that the creator has access to or knowledge of both groups, giving them comparative insight. It works because people are naturally curious about what makes high achievers different, and the promise of identifying specific differences makes excellence feel achievable rather than mysterious.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

If the differences identified are too obvious (like "top performers work harder") or too vague (like "they have better mindset"), the hook disappoints. The comparison must reveal genuinely non-obvious or counter-intuitive differences that explain performance gaps. If the creator doesn't have credible access to or knowledge of top performers, the claim rings hollow. The differences must be actionable and within the control of the viewer—if they're based on resources, connections, or circumstances most people lack, the insight isn't helpful. The hook can also fail if it creates too stark a binary between "top performers" and "everyone else," when reality is more of a spectrum.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

The comparison can make average performers feel inadequate or judged, creating negative emotional associations with the creator. It may inadvertently suggest that if you're not a "top performer," you're in the "everyone else" category, which can be demotivating. The hook can attract criticism about survivorship bias—focusing on what successful people do without accounting for luck, timing, or external factors. If the differences highlighted are personality-based or require fundamental changes to who someone is, viewers may feel excluded rather than empowered. The binary framing can oversimplify complex success factors and ignore the role of privilege, resources, or circumstances. If viewers adopt the "different" behaviors but don't become top performers, they may blame the creator for false promises.
