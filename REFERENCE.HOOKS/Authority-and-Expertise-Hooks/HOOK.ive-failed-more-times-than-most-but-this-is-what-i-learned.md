# I've failed more times than most, but this is what I learned.

## Hook Group

Authority & Expertise Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

The viewer should perceive the creator as someone who has extensive experience with failure, giving them hard-won credibility and pattern recognition about what doesn't work and what eventually does, making success feel more accessible through persistence.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

Viewers should feel:

- Validation that failure is part of the success journey
- Relief that struggling doesn't mean permanent defeat
- Curiosity about lessons extracted from extensive failure
- Trust in wisdom earned through repeated setbacks
- Motivation to persist through their own failures
- Connection with someone who admits vulnerability and struggle

## Psychology

Failure narratives create powerful connection because most people experience more failure than success and feel alone in it. Admitting extensive failure humanizes the expert while actually enhancing credibility—they've been tested repeatedly and survived, suggesting resilience and learning capacity. "More than most" creates a surprising inversion—usually people claim more success than others, but claiming more failure positions the creator as exceptionally experienced through adversity. This taps into our respect for persistence and our belief that failure teaches better than success. It also addresses imposter syndrome by normalizing struggle.

## Example

"I've failed more times than most, but this is what I learned: momentum beats perfection every single time."

## Why it works

This hook disarms viewers by leading with vulnerability instead of accomplishment, creating immediate trust and relatability. The extensive failure claim paradoxically enhances credibility—they've survived enough failures to learn patterns others miss. "But this is what I learned" reframes failure as valuable education rather than defeat, making struggle feel purposeful. It positions the creator as battle-tested and resilient, qualities that command respect. The format also creates a safe space for viewers struggling with their own failures, building a community around growth through adversity. It suggests the creator's current success is earned through persistence, making it feel more authentic and replicable.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails when the failures mentioned are trivial, easily overcome, or not actually failures, making the claim feel performative or attention-seeking. It loses credibility if the creator can't demonstrate they've actually recovered and succeeded after these failures—continued struggling without eventual success doesn't validate the learning. If the lesson extracted from extensive failure is too obvious or doesn't clearly connect to overcoming those failures, it disappoints. The hook also underperforms when it dwells on failure without pivoting to insight, creating discouragement rather than learning. When the vulnerability feels calculated or strategic rather than authentic, it damages trust.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This hook can inadvertently position the creator as someone who fails frequently, raising questions about judgment, competence, or whether their advice is reliable if they struggle so much. It may attract a victim mentality audience that bonds over failure rather than growth, creating a community focused on commiseration instead of progress. The emphasis on failure can overshadow current success, defining the creator by their struggles rather than achievements. If viewers follow the advice and still fail repeatedly, they may question whether the creator's path was just survivorship bias. The hook can also make success seem impossibly difficult, requiring extensive failure, when some people might succeed more directly.
