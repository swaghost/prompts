# Everyone is Using [Trend], But Almost Nobody is Doing it Correctly

## Hook Group

Trend Hooks, Contrarian Hooks, Expertise Hooks, Correction Hooks

## Desired Viewer Perception

Viewers should perceive this as insider expertise that will help them stand out in a crowded trend. The creator should feel like someone who understands the deeper mechanics behind why trends work, not just surface-level copying.

## Desired Viewer Emotional/Intellectual/Psychological/Physical Elicitations or Response

**Emotional:** Frustration at wasted effort on wrong execution, hope that correct execution will yield results, relief at getting expert guidance

**Intellectual:** Curiosity about the correct approach, analysis of own trend usage, pattern recognition of common mistakes

**Psychological:** Validation if they suspected something was off, desire for competitive advantage, trust in expert correction

**Physical:** Attention lock for the reveal, potential relief if they haven't tried the trend yet, note-taking for correct approach

## Psychology

This hook exploits the psychological tension between FOMO (everyone's using this trend) and desire for differentiation (I need to do it better than others). By acknowledging that a trend is popular while claiming most people misuse it, you position yourself as someone who sees beneath surface patterns to underlying principles. The "almost nobody" phrasing is key—it suggests the correct approach exists but isn't obvious, creating information asymmetry you're about to resolve. This hook also validates viewers who tried the trend and didn't see results; instead of feeling like failures, they can attribute poor performance to incorrect execution, which feels fixable. The hook leverages our preference for being among the knowledgeable few rather than the ignorant many—by teaching the "correct" way, you help viewers join an elite group who "get it." The trend specificity makes the content feel timely and relevant rather than generic evergreen advice.

## Example

"Everyone is using AI for content, but almost nobody is doing it correctly. They're generating, not augmenting. Here's the difference."

"Everyone is using POV hooks, but almost nobody is doing it correctly. Without specificity, they're just noise."

"Everyone is using carousel posts, but almost nobody is doing it correctly. Slide one needs to do THIS, not that."

## Why it works

This hook succeeds by acknowledging trend popularity (validating that it matters) while promising superior execution (differentiating you from other trend-chasers). It works because it transforms passive trend-following into strategic trend-leveraging—you're not just copying, you're understanding. The hook also creates urgency; if everyone else is doing it wrong, getting it right represents competitive advantage while the window exists. The correction framing positions you as expert rather than peer; you're not discovering the trend alongside everyone else, you've already mastered it and identified common pitfalls. The hook works across experience levels: people who haven't tried the trend yet feel they're getting ahead start, people who tried and failed feel validated and ready to course-correct, people doing well feel curious whether they could do even better. The "almost nobody" framing also makes viewers wonder if they're among the rare correct executors or part of the problematic majority, creating self-assessment curiosity.

## Pitfalls - How it can fail

The hook fails if your "correct" approach isn't actually meaningfully different or better than common usage. If you criticize how others use a trend but your alternative is marginal improvement or personal preference rather than genuine upgrade, credibility collapses. It also fails if you're wrong—if your "correct" approach is actually less effective than what most people are doing, you'll look foolish and lose authority. The hook doesn't work if the trend you're addressing is already over or was never actually popular—claiming "everyone" is using something viewers have barely seen makes you seem out of touch. Another failure mode: if the "correct" way requires significantly more effort, resources, or expertise than typical approaches, viewers may conclude the standard way is "good enough" for them and ignore your advice. The hook also falls flat if you don't clearly demonstrate why typical usage is wrong and what makes your approach correct—staying in critique mode without clear improvement teaching wastes the setup. Finally, if you use this hook pattern repeatedly, viewers may see it as a formula ("X is popular but everyone's wrong") rather than genuine expertise, reducing trust.

## Unintended Consequences - How it can backfire

This hook can accidentally create pedantic, correctness-obsessed audiences who police each other about "proper" trend usage, potentially making your community feel judge-y rather than supportive. It can also discourage experimentation; if viewers believe there's one "correct" way to use trends, they may not try personal variations that could work even better for their specific context. The hook might trigger defensiveness in viewers who are using the trend the "wrong" way, making them feel criticized rather than helped—especially if they've seen some success with their approach. By positioning common usage as incorrect, you might also undermine confidence in viewers' judgment, creating dependency on your approval for validation. The hook can also age poorly; if your "correct" approach becomes the new standard everyone copies, your differentiation disappears and the advice becomes obvious. Additionally, by focusing on trend execution rather than original creation, you might inadvertently reinforce copycat culture instead of encouraging unique content. The correction framing can also make you seem arrogant or condescending if not executed with humility—claiming almost everyone is wrong positions you as superior, which can alienate as easily as it attracts.
