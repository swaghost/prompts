# Anti-AI Writing Patterns Guide

## Purpose

This is a practical reference for spotting and revising recognizable AI-writing patterns. It is not an AI detector and it cannot prove who wrote a passage. A single word, punctuation mark, heading style, or polished sentence is not meaningful evidence by itself. Look for clusters, genre mismatch, repeated structures, unsupported claims, and exposed tool artifacts.

Source basis: [Wikipedia: Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), accessed 2026-08-20. The source page is descriptive guidance, not a policy, and warns against treating these observations as definitive proof.

## The 20 Patterns

### 1. Inflated significance

The text turns an ordinary fact into a claim about a pivotal moment, broader movement, lasting legacy, or changing landscape.

Example: "The shop opened in 2019, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of local retail."

Correction: State the fact and its direct consequence. Delete significance that the evidence does not support.

### 2. Canned notability and media coverage

The text gestures toward "independent coverage," "leading outlets," or an "active social media presence" to imply importance without explaining what the sources actually establish.

Example: "The company has been profiled by leading industry and regional publications."

Correction: Name the source and the specific fact it supports, or remove the claim.

### 3. Superficial analysis

A vague present-participle phrase claims that something "highlights," "underscores," "reflects," "fosters," or "contributes to" a broad idea without adding evidence.

Example: "The new logo uses blue, reflecting the brand's commitment to trust and innovation."

Correction: Explain the observable design choice or omit the interpretation.

### 4. Promotional or advertisement-like language

Neutral content is padded with words such as vibrant, rich, groundbreaking, renowned, breathtaking, seamless, showcases, commitment to, or in the heart of.

Example: "This vibrant destination offers a breathtaking tapestry of culture."

Correction: Replace praise with specific, verifiable description.

### 5. Vague attribution and overgeneralized opinion

Claims are assigned to "experts," "observers," "industry reports," "critics," or "several sources" without identifying who said what.

Example: "Experts agree that the policy represents a major breakthrough."

Correction: Name the speaker or source, qualify the claim, or remove it.

### 6. Formulaic challenges and future prospects

A rigid ending introduces "challenges," "despite these challenges," "future prospects," or "ongoing initiatives" and closes with vague optimism.

Example: "Despite these challenges, the organization is well positioned for future growth."

Correction: State the actual unresolved problem, decision, or next event. Do not manufacture a hopeful ending.

### 7. Generic AI vocabulary density

The passage clusters words such as delve, crucial, pivotal, robust, intricate, landscape, tapestry, testament, foster, enhance, showcase, underscore, highlight, align, and valuable insights.

Example: "This robust framework enhances alignment across the broader landscape."

Correction: Use the plain verb or noun that describes the actual action.

### 8. Avoidance of simple is, are, has, and have

Simple copular or possessive sentences are replaced by inflated constructions such as "serves as," "stands as," "features," "offers," "maintains," or "represents."

Example: "The document serves as a guide that features three sections."

Correction: "The document is a guide with three sections."

### 9. Vague connection language

The text uses "in connection with," "associated with," or "in relation to" where a direct relationship would be clearer.

Example: "The award was associated with the project's environmental work."

Correction: "The project won the award for its environmental work," if that is supported.

### 10. Negative parallelism

The prose repeatedly uses "not just X, but Y," "not X, but Y," "not only X, but also Y," or "X rather than Y" as a dramatic contrast.

Example: "It is not just a tool, but a transformation."

Correction: State the useful claim directly or keep the contrast only when the distinction matters.

### 11. Rule-of-three overuse

Ideas, adjectives, benefits, or conclusions repeatedly arrive in groups of three, creating a canned rhythm.

Example: "It is faster, smarter, and more effective."

Correction: Keep a three-part list when it reflects the content. Otherwise choose the strongest item or vary the structure.

### 12. Generic title heading and rigid outline

The response begins by repeating the title as a heading, uses a predictable sequence of headings, or includes headings that contain no prose of their own.

Example: A page titled "Pricing Strategy" begins with a large "Pricing Strategy" heading, followed by generic sections such as "Overview," "Benefits," "Challenges," and "Future Outlook."

Correction: Start with the content when the surrounding interface already supplies the title. Use only headings that help the reader navigate real material.

### 13. Title Case everywhere

Every heading capitalizes all major words even when the publication's house style uses sentence case.

Example: "Key Benefits And Strategic Considerations"

Correction: Match the target publication's heading style.

### 14. Over-formatting

The text uses boldface, emojis, inline-header lists, decorative separators, or small tables mechanically rather than because the format improves comprehension.

Example: "**Key takeaway:** **The** **system** **improves** **everything**."

Correction: Use formatting sparingly and let the content determine the structure.

### 15. Overuse of em dashes

Em dashes appear repeatedly, especially with spaces, to create a punched-up sales rhythm or artificial emphasis.

Example: "The result - a faster process - with fewer errors - and better outcomes."

Correction: Use a period, comma, colon, parentheses, or a properly placed dash where the pause is genuinely needed.

### 16. Emoji as formatting

Emojis decorate headings, bullets, or section labels without adding meaning.

Example: "🚀 Growth Strategy" and "✅ Key Takeaway" throughout a formal report.

Correction: Remove them or keep one only when the audience and medium genuinely call for it.

### 17. Placeholder text and phrasal templates

Unfilled brackets, generic variables, placeholder dates, or instructions to add missing information remain in the final copy.

Example: "Our service helps [TARGET AUDIENCE] achieve [DESIRED OUTCOME]."

Correction: Fill the field from verified input or remove the sentence.

### 18. Exposed internal or citation markup

The text contains artifacts such as contentReference, oaicite, turn0search0, [cite: 1], start_span, attached_file, grok_card, dagger citation brackets, or other tool-specific tokens.

Example: "The study found a 20% increase. [cite: 1]"

Correction: Resolve the citation into a real source or remove the broken artifact. Never silently preserve it.

### 19. Unsupported citations and citation theater

The text includes broken links, invalid DOIs or ISBNs, unrelated sources, unused references, UTM parameters from an AI tool, or book citations that cannot verify the claim.

Example: A DOI resolves to a different paper than the one cited.

Correction: Verify every citation against the claim. Mark an item unverified when it cannot be checked.

### 20. Canned assistant communication

The response begins or ends with stock phrases such as "I hope this helps," "Certainly," "You're absolutely right," "Here is a detailed breakdown," "Would you like me to," or "Let me know if you need anything else."

Example: "Certainly! Here is a comprehensive overview. I hope this helps."

Correction: Start with the answer and stop when the useful work is complete.

## Do Not Treat These as Proof

The following are weak or ineffective indicators on their own:

- Perfect grammar
- Formal or academic prose in general
- A few transition words
- Markdown by itself
- An unsourced claim by itself
- A bland or robotic feeling
- Curly quotation marks alone
- One em dash
- A mixed formal and casual register

## Revision Rules

1. Diagnose clusters, not isolated tells.
2. Preserve facts, meaning, deliberate formatting, and the user's legitimate voice.
3. Delete unsupported significance, praise, attribution, and speculation instead of replacing them with different filler.
4. Use direct, ordinary constructions when they are clearer.
5. Keep a pattern when the genre, audience, or writer's established style justifies it.
6. Never invent personal experience, evidence, citations, or a point of view to make prose seem human.
7. Report uncertainty. A revision can reduce recognizable patterns without proving anything about authorship.
