# Red Ocean vs Blue Ocean Strategy

## The Complete Strategic Framework for Market Creation

Developed by W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne at INSEAD, the Red Ocean vs Blue Ocean framework redefines how we think about competition and market creation.

---

## The Core Distinction

### Red Ocean Strategy: Competing in Existing Market Space

**Definition:** Fighting for share in known, crowded markets where competitors battle for the same customers using the same rules.

**Characteristics:**

- 🩸 **Bloody competition:** Fight for market share
- 💰 **Margin erosion:** Price wars and commoditization
- 📊 **Zero-sum game:** One company's gain = another's loss
- 🔄 **Incremental thinking:** Small improvements on existing offerings
- ⚖️ **Value-cost trade-off:** Choose low cost OR differentiation
- 🎯 **Beat the competition:** Strategic focus on outperforming rivals

**Visual:**

```
Red Ocean = Existing Market Space
┌─────────────────────────────┐
│  Competitor A  Competitor B │
│     You    Competitor C     │
│  Competitor D  Competitor E │
│  Fighting for same customers│
│  Shrinking margins          │
│  Commoditization            │
└─────────────────────────────┘
```

---

### Blue Ocean Strategy: Creating Uncontested Market Space

**Definition:** Making competition irrelevant by creating and capturing new demand in markets that don't yet exist.

**Characteristics:**

- 🌊 **Uncontested market:** Create new demand
- 💎 **Value innovation:** Differentiation AND low cost
- 📈 **Growing pie:** Create new customers, not just steal share
- 🚀 **Quantum leaps:** Transformational thinking
- 🎁 **Break trade-off:** Pursue differentiation AND low cost simultaneously
- 🏆 **Make competition irrelevant:** Strategic focus on new value creation

**Visual:**

```
Blue Ocean = Uncontested New Market Space
┌─────────────────────────────┐
│         You (alone)         │
│    New customer segment     │
│    Strong margins           │
│    Rapid growth             │
│    No direct competition    │
└─────────────────────────────┘
```

---

## The Strategic Comparison

| **Dimension**            | **Red Ocean Strategy**                                  | **Blue Ocean Strategy**                                       |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Market Space**         | Compete in existing market                              | Create uncontested market space                               |
| **Competition**          | Beat the competition                                    | Make competition irrelevant                                   |
| **Demand**               | Exploit existing demand                                 | Create and capture new demand                                 |
| **Value-Cost Trade-Off** | Make value-cost trade-off (low cost OR differentiation) | Break value-cost trade-off (low cost AND differentiation)     |
| **System Alignment**     | Align activities with strategic choice                  | Align whole system pursuing both differentiation and low cost |
| **Strategic Focus**      | Outperform rivals                                       | Value innovation                                              |
| **Growth Dynamics**      | Zero-sum (share stealing)                               | Positive-sum (market creation)                                |
| **Profit Potential**     | Declining margins                                       | Superior profit dynamics                                      |

## Why Red Oceans Turn Bloody

### The Commoditization Trap

**The Downward Spiral:**

```
1. New market emerges (Blue Ocean)
         ↓
2. Success attracts competitors
         ↓
3. Market becomes crowded (Red Ocean forming)
         ↓
4. Differentiation becomes expensive
         ↓
5. Companies compete on price
         ↓
6. Margins erode
         ↓
7. Innovation becomes incremental
         ↓
8. Market commoditizes
         ↓
9. Only low-cost players survive
```

### Red Ocean Symptoms

**You're in a Red Ocean if:**

- ❌ Customers primarily compare you on price
- ❌ Differentiation is temporary (competitors copy quickly)
- ❌ Margins are shrinking year-over-year
- ❌ Innovation feels incremental (slightly better specs)
- ❌ Growth comes only from taking competitors' customers
- ❌ Strategic conversations focus on competitive responses
- ❌ Value proposition sounds similar to competitors
- ❌ Sales cycles involve feature comparison charts

### The Incremental Thinking Trap

**Red Ocean companies:**

- Add more features (but so do competitors)
- Lower prices (but competitors match)
- Increase advertising (but noise increases)
- Improve service (but it's expected)
- Benchmark competitors (and perpetuate sameness)

**Result:** Running faster to stay in place.

## The Blue Ocean Opportunity

### What Blue Oceans Look Like

**Characteristics of successful Blue Ocean moves:**

**1. New Value Proposition:**

- Offers something fundamentally different
- Not "better" on existing terms, but "different" on new terms
- Appeals to customers who weren't served before

**2. Profitable Growth:**

- Strong margins (less price sensitivity)
- Rapid customer acquisition (new demand)
- Sustainable advantage (hard to copy the whole system)

**3. Strategic Clarity:**

- Simple, clear positioning
- Easy to understand and communicate
- Resonates with target customer immediately

**4. Competition Irrelevance:**

- No direct comparables
- Creating new category or sub-category
- Customers can't easily find substitutes

### Blue Ocean Examples

**Classic Cases:**

**1. Cirque du Soleil:**

- **Red Ocean:** Traditional circus industry (declining, cost pressure, star performers, animal shows)
- **Blue Ocean Move:** Created "artistic theater" category
- **What they did:**
  - **Eliminated:** Animal shows, star performers, multiple rings, aisle concession sales
  - **Reduced:** Humor, thrills, danger
  - **Raised:** Unique venue, refined environment
  - **Created:** Theme, artistic music and dance, multiple productions
- **Result:** Adults willing to pay theater prices for circus experience, premium margins, global brand

---

**2. Southwest Airlines:**

- **Red Ocean:** Full-service airlines competing on routes, frequency, and service levels
- **Blue Ocean Move:** Created "convenient low-cost air travel" category
- **What they did:**
  - **Eliminated:** Meals, seat assignments, hub-and-spoke routing, multiple classes
  - **Reduced:** Lounges, frequent flyer programs
  - **Raised:** Friendly service, flight frequency, speed
  - **Created:** Point-to-point routing between secondary airports
- **Result:** Price-sensitive travelers who would have driven or taken a bus, profitable for 40+ consecutive years

---

**3. Nintendo Wii:**

- **Red Ocean:** Gaming consoles competing on graphics, processing power, hardcore games
- **Blue Ocean Move:** Created "family fun gaming" category
- **What they did:**
  - **Eliminated:** High-definition graphics, hard drive, complex controllers
  - **Reduced:** Game variety (focused on family-friendly)
  - **Raised:** Physical interaction, ease of play
  - **Created:** Motion sensing, family gaming together, non-gamers as customers
- **Result:** Grandparents, parents, and kids playing together; outsold Xbox 360 and PlayStation 3

---

**4. Yellow Tail Wine:**

- **Red Ocean:** Wine industry with complex terminology, prestige hierarchy, intimidating selection
- **Blue Ocean Move:** Created "accessible fun wine" category
- **What they did:**
  - **Eliminated:** Enological terminology, aging qualities, prestige imagery
  - **Reduced:** Wine complexity, range of varieties
  - **Raised:** Easy drinking, easy selection, price
  - **Created:** Fun/adventure, ease of selection for non-wine drinkers, retail shelf impact
- **Result:** Beer drinkers and cocktail drinkers buying wine, became fastest-growing brand in U.S. and Australia

---

**5. IKEA:**

- **Red Ocean:** Furniture retailers competing on selection, delivery, and service
- **Blue Ocean Move:** Created "affordable design furniture with instant gratification" category
- **What they did:**
  - **Eliminated:** Assembly service, salespeople on floor, delivery (initially)
  - **Reduced:** Variety within product lines
  - **Raised:** Store design (showrooms), self-service, in-store childcare
  - **Created:** Modular design, flat-pack take-home, Swedish design aesthetic at low prices
- **Result:** Young couples and students who couldn't afford design furniture, global brand

## How To Shift From Red to Blue Ocean

### The Four Actions Framework

**The core tool for creating Blue Ocean strategy:**

Use these four questions to reconstruct market boundaries:

#### 1. ELIMINATE

**Question:** _"Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?"_

**Purpose:** Remove costly factors that don't create value for customers.

**Examples:**

- Cirque du Soleil eliminated animal shows (expensive, activists opposed)
- Southwest eliminated meals and seat assignments (costly, customers didn't care)
- IKEA eliminated in-home delivery and assembly (customers willing to trade for price)
- Yellow Tail eliminated wine aging prestige and complexity (intimidating to non-wine drinkers)

**How to identify:**

- What do you do just because competitors do it?
- What adds cost but doesn't add value to customers?
- What industry "must-haves" aren't really necessary?

---

#### 2. REDUCE

**Question:** _"Which factors should be reduced well below the industry's standard?"_

**Purpose:** Cut back on over-served dimensions to reduce cost and complexity.

**Examples:**

- Cirque du Soleil reduced humor and thrills (vs. traditional circus)
- Southwest reduced airport lounge access and frequent flyer perks
- IKEA reduced variety within product lines (limited color/style options)
- Nintendo Wii reduced graphics quality and processing power

**How to identify:**

- What are you over-delivering on?
- Where are customers satisfied with "good enough"?
- What complexity can be removed?

---

#### 3. RAISE

**Question:** _"Which factors should be raised well above the industry's standard?"_

**Purpose:** Over-deliver on factors that create disproportionate value.

**Examples:**

- Cirque du Soleil raised artistic music/dance and refined environment
- Southwest raised flight frequency and friendly service
- IKEA raised showroom experience and store design
- Peloton raised instructor quality and live class experience

**How to identify:**

- What matters more to customers than industry realizes?
- Where can over-delivery create loyalty?
- What would customers pay a premium for?

---

#### 4. CREATE

**Question:** _"Which factors should be created that the industry has never offered?"_

**Purpose:** Unlock new sources of value.

**Examples:**

- Cirque du Soleil created theatrical themes and multiple productions
- Southwest created point-to-point routing to secondary airports
- IKEA created flat-pack take-home instant gratification
- Peloton created at-home live classes with leaderboard community
- Yellow Tail created fun/adventure positioning for wine

**How to identify:**

- What do customers wish existed but doesn't?
- What adjacent customer needs are unmet?
- What new sources of value can be unlocked?

### The Strategy Canvas

**Visual tool to map competitive factors and identify Blue Ocean opportunities:**

```
High │         X Competitor A
     │      X     X Competitor B
     │   X           X You (current)
     │X                 X
Low  └───────────────────────────────
     Price  Quality  Service  Selection  Experience
```

**How to use it:**

**Step 1: List Competitive Factors**

- What does the industry compete on?
- What do customers evaluate when choosing?
- List 8-12 key factors

**Step 2: Plot Competitor Positions**

- Rate each competitor on each factor (low to high)
- Include yourself
- Draw connecting lines to visualize "value curves"

**Step 3: Identify Red Ocean Clustering**

- Where do all competitors look similar?
- Where is everyone competing on the same factors?
- That's the Red Ocean

**Step 4: Apply Four Actions Framework**

- Which factors to eliminate or reduce?
- Which factors to raise?
- What new factors to create?

**Step 5: Draw Your New Value Curve**

- Plot your new strategic profile
- It should look distinctly different from competitors
- Lower in some places (eliminate/reduce)
- Higher in others (raise)
- New factors that didn't exist before (create)

**Goal:** Your value curve should diverge from competitors, creating a unique strategic profile.

### The Value Innovation Concept

**Core Principle:** Pursue differentiation AND low cost simultaneously.

**Traditional thinking (Red Ocean):**

- You must choose: Low cost OR differentiation
- Can't do both
- Strategy = choosing one or the other

**Blue Ocean thinking:**

- Break the value-cost trade-off
- By eliminating and reducing low-value factors, you reduce cost
- By raising and creating high-value factors, you increase differentiation
- Value innovation = both at the same time

**The Value Innovation Model:**

```
         VALUE INNOVATION
              ↗️    ↖️
        Buyers        Costs
        ↑              ↓
    Raise/Create   Eliminate/Reduce
    High-Value     Low-Value
     Factors        Factors
```

**Example: Cirque du Soleil**

- **Costs Down:** Eliminated expensive star performers, animal care, multiple venues
- **Value Up:** Created theatrical storytelling, refined environment, unique productions
- **Result:** Lower cost structure than traditional circus + premium pricing from theater audience

## Strategic Paths to Blue Oceans

### 1. Look Across Alternative Industries

**Instead of comparing to direct competitors, look at alternatives customers consider.**

**Questions:**

- What do customers use instead of your product?
- What alternative industries solve similar problems?
- What can you learn from them?

**Example:**

- **NetJets:** Looked across alternatives (commercial airlines, charter jets, full ownership) and created fractional jet ownership
- **Starbucks:** Looked at bars and lounges (social gathering) not just coffee shops, created "third place" between home and work

---

### 2. Look Across Strategic Groups

**Within an industry, look at different strategic groups (luxury, mid-market, budget).**

**Questions:**

- What if you combined elements from different groups?
- Can you offer some luxury features at a budget price?
- Can you simplify a premium offering?

**Example:**

- **Curves:** Combined convenience of local gyms (strategic group 1) with support of weight-loss programs (strategic group 2) for time-constrained women
- **Ralph Lauren:** Combined luxury fashion design with accessible pricing

---

### 3. Look Across the Chain of Buyers

**Focus on a different buyer in the value chain.**

**Questions:**

- Who are all the people involved in the purchase decision?
- What if you focused on a different person?
- What do they value that others overlook?

**Example:**

- **Novo Nordisk (insulin pens):** Shifted focus from doctors (traditional buyer) to patients (actual users), created easy-to-use insulin delivery
- **Bloomberg:** Focused on traders (users) not just IT departments (purchasers), created trader-centric terminals

---

### 4. Look Across Complementary Products

**Consider the total solution customers need (including before, during, and after use).**

**Questions:**

- What happens before, during, and after customers use your product?
- What complementary products do they need?
- What pain points exist in the total experience?

**Example:**

- **NABI (bus manufacturer):** Addressed bus driver turnover (complementary issue to bus purchase) by creating buses that were safer and easier to drive
- **Philips (home defibrillators):** Addressed emergency response context, created simple devices for non-medical users

---

### 5. Look Across Functional or Emotional Appeal

**Shift from functional to emotional or vice versa.**

**Questions:**

- Is your industry competing on functional or emotional appeal?
- What if you flipped it?
- Can you add emotion to a functional industry or simplify an emotional one?

**Example:**

- **The Body Shop:** Added emotional appeal (environmental values, natural ingredients) to functional cosmetics industry
- **Swatch:** Added emotional appeal (fashion, collectibility) to functional watch industry
- **QB House (Japan):** Removed emotional appeal from haircuts, created functional 10-minute cuts

---

### 6. Look Across Time

**Consider major trends and how they'll reshape value.**

**Questions:**

- What trends are irreversible?
- How will they change what customers value?
- Can you act on that insight now?

**Example:**

- **Apple iTunes:** Anticipated digital music trend, created legal download marketplace before industry shifted
- **Tesla:** Anticipated EV trend and charging infrastructure need, built Supercharger network early

## Implementing Blue Ocean Strategy

### Step 1: Select the Right Scope

**Where to focus your Blue Ocean effort:**

**Option A: Existing Industry, New Segment**

- Stay in your industry
- Target non-customers or underserved segments
- Example: Cirque du Soleil (circus industry, adult audience)

**Option B: Existing Customers, New Value**

- Keep your customers
- Offer radically different value proposition
- Example: Southwest (air travelers, point-to-point convenience)

**Option C: Adjacent Market, New Offering**

- Move into adjacent space
- Combine elements from multiple industries
- Example: Starbucks (coffee + social gathering "third place")

---

### Step 2: Understand Non-Customers

**Three tiers of non-customers:**

**Tier 1: Soon-to-be non-customers**

- People on the edge of your market
- Use your product minimally
- Ready to leave if better alternative appears
- **Opportunity:** What would keep them engaged?

**Tier 2: Refusing non-customers**

- People who consciously reject your market
- Have considered and said "no"
- Use alternatives or go without
- **Opportunity:** What barrier can you remove?

**Tier 3: Unexplored non-customers**

- People who've never considered your market
- Seem distant from your industry
- Largest pool
- **Opportunity:** What new value would attract them?

**Example: Callaway Big Bertha Golf Clubs**

- **Tier 1:** Occasional golfers frustrated by difficulty
- **Tier 2:** Former golfers who quit due to poor performance
- **Tier 3:** Non-golfers intimidated by the sport
- **Solution:** Oversized club head made hitting easier, brought millions of new golfers into the market

---

### Step 3: Get the Strategic Sequence Right

**Four questions to validate your Blue Ocean move:**

```
1. Is there exceptional utility?
      ↓ YES
2. Is the strategic pricing attractive?
      ↓ YES
3. Can you achieve target cost?
      ↓ YES
4. What are adoption hurdles?
      ↓ ADDRESSED
   Blue Ocean Launch
```

**1. Buyer Utility:**

- Does it solve a major pain point?
- Is the value clear and compelling?
- Will customers immediately understand the benefit?

**2. Strategic Pricing:**

- What are customers paying for alternatives?
- What price attracts mass of target buyers?
- Can you be profitable at that price?

**3. Target Costing:**

- Working backward from strategic price
- Can you achieve target margin at that price?
- What must you eliminate/reduce to hit cost target?

**4. Adoption:**

- What barriers to adoption exist?
- Regulatory, behavioral, organizational resistance?
- How will you address each barrier?

---

### Step 4: Build Execution Into Strategy

**Blue Ocean strategy requires organizational alignment:**

**Overcome Four Organizational Hurdles:**

**1. Cognitive Hurdle:**

- Help people see the need for change
- Make them experience the current pain
- Show Blue Ocean opportunity clearly

**2. Resource Hurdle:**

- Focus resources on hot spots (highest impact areas)
- Reallocate from cold spots (low impact areas)
- Do more with existing resources

**3. Motivational Hurdle:**

- Focus on kingpins (key influencers)
- Make expectations clear and transparent
- Celebrate early wins

**4. Political Hurdle:**

- Identify opponents and supporters
- Build coalitions
- Secure support from angels (senior champions)
- Silence devils (vocal opponents)

## Common Blue Ocean Mistakes

### 1. Technology-Driven Innovation (Not Value-Driven)

**Mistake:** Build cutting-edge technology and hope customers buy.

**Reality:** Value innovation focuses on buyer value, not technology leadership.

**Fix:** Start with customer pain points, not technology capabilities.

**Example:**

- ❌ "We have the fastest processor" (technology-driven)
- ✅ "We make video editing instant" (value-driven)

---

### 2. Over-Segmentation

**Mistake:** Slice market into tiny niches, miss mass appeal.

**Reality:** Blue Oceans target the mass of buyers, not narrow segments.

**Fix:** Find commonalities across buyers, not differences.

**Example:**

- ❌ Cirque du Soleil targeting "affluent theater-goers age 35-50 in urban areas"
- ✅ Cirque du Soleil targeting "adults seeking sophisticated entertainment"

---

### 3. Copying Existing Value Curves

**Mistake:** Benchmarking competitors and trying to "beat" them on existing factors.

**Reality:** Blue Ocean is about divergence, not benchmarking.

**Fix:** Focus on creating new value curve, not improving existing one.

---

### 4. Conflating Innovation with Value Innovation

**Mistake:** Assume any innovation creates a Blue Ocean.

**Reality:** Only value innovation (simultaneous differentiation + low cost) creates Blue Oceans.

**Fix:** Test whether innovation reduces cost while increasing value.

---

### 5. Mistaking Niche for Blue Ocean

**Mistake:** Creating offering for tiny, specialized market.

**Reality:** Blue Oceans have mass appeal, niches are small by definition.

**Fix:** Ensure your Blue Ocean targets a large pool of buyers.

## Blue Ocean Sustainability

### How Long Do Blue Oceans Last?

**Reality:** Blue Oceans eventually attract competitors and turn red.

**Timeline varies:**

- Some last decades (Southwest: 40+ years)
- Some last years before imitation
- Some fade as customer needs evolve

### Building Moats

**How to sustain Blue Ocean advantage:**

**1. Continuous Value Innovation:**

- Don't rest on initial Blue Ocean
- Keep applying Four Actions Framework
- Innovate before competitors catch up

**2. Build Network Effects:**

- Make product better with more users
- Creates barrier to entry
- Example: eBay marketplace, LinkedIn network

**3. Create Switching Costs:**

- Lock-in through data, habits, integrations
- Example: Salesforce (CRM data lock-in)

**4. Build Brand and Culture:**

- Strong brand becomes hard to replicate
- Culture of innovation sustains advantage
- Example: Apple ecosystem and brand loyalty

**5. Align Whole System:**

- Blue Ocean requires system changes
- Harder to copy entire system than one feature
- Example: IKEA's entire business model (flat-pack, showroom, self-service)

### When to Create a New Blue Ocean

**Signs you need a new Blue Ocean move:**

- Your Blue Ocean is turning red (competitors catching up)
- Margins are eroding
- Growth is slowing
- Customer switching is increasing
- Innovation feels incremental again

**Blue Ocean is not a one-time move—it's a continuous mindset.**

## Blue Ocean vs. Disruption Theory

### How They Differ

**Disruption Theory (Clayton Christensen):**

- Focus on how new technologies disrupt incumbents
- Typically lower quality initially, improves over time
- Targets over-served or non-consumers
- Incumbent's dilemma: can't respond without cannibalizing

**Blue Ocean Strategy:**

- Focus on creating new market space through value innovation
- Not necessarily about technology
- Simultaneous differentiation AND low cost
- Makes competition irrelevant, not just disrupts incumbents

### Both Can Coexist

**Disruptive innovation can create Blue Oceans:**

- Example: Netflix (disruption + Blue Ocean)
- Started as low-end disruption (DVD by mail)
- Created Blue Ocean (streaming entertainment)

**Blue Oceans don't always disrupt:**

- Example: Cirque du Soleil didn't disrupt circus industry
- Created new market space (adult entertainment)
- Traditional circuses continued serving kids

## Applying Blue Ocean to Your Business

### The 30-Day Blue Ocean Challenge

**Week 1: Diagnose**

- [ ] Map your industry's competitive factors (Strategy Canvas)
- [ ] Plot yourself and key competitors
- [ ] Identify Red Ocean clustering
- [ ] List symptoms of commoditization (margin pressure, price wars, etc.)

**Week 2: Explore**

- [ ] Interview non-customers (all three tiers)
- [ ] Study pain points in customer journey
- [ ] Look across alternative industries
- [ ] Apply Six Paths Framework (alternatives, strategic groups, buyers, complements, appeal, time)

**Week 3: Innovate**

- [ ] Apply Four Actions Framework (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create)
- [ ] Draw new value curve
- [ ] Test whether it breaks value-cost trade-off
- [ ] Pressure-test with potential customers

**Week 4: Validate**

- [ ] Run buyer utility test (is value clear?)
- [ ] Determine strategic pricing (what will they pay?)
- [ ] Calculate target cost (can we profit at that price?)
- [ ] Identify adoption hurdles (what's in the way?)

### Blue Ocean Thinking Questions

**Ask yourself regularly:**

1. **"What industry factors do we compete on just because everyone else does?"**
   - What can we eliminate?

2. **"What are we over-delivering on that customers don't truly value?"**
   - What can we reduce?

3. **"What do customers care about more than the industry realizes?"**
   - What should we raise?

4. **"What do customers wish existed but no one offers?"**
   - What can we create?

5. **"Who doesn't buy from our industry and why?"**
   - How can we remove barriers for non-customers?

6. **"What if we could break the value-cost trade-off?"**
   - How can we reduce cost AND increase value?

## The Bottom Line

### Red Ocean Reality

**Staying in Red Oceans = slow decline:**

- Margins erode
- Differentiation becomes expensive
- Commoditization is inevitable
- Growth comes only from stealing share
- Strategic conversations focus on defensive moves

### Blue Ocean Imperative

**Creating Blue Oceans = profitable growth:**

- Strong margins
- Competition irrelevance
- New demand creation
- Sustainable advantage
- Strategic conversations focus on value innovation

### The Choice

**Every business faces this choice:**

- Compete in Red Oceans (fight for share)
- Create Blue Oceans (create new demand)

**The best strategists do both:**

- Manage current business in Red Oceans
- Systematically create Blue Oceans for future growth

### Remember

**Competition is a choice.**

The goal isn't to beat competition—it's to make competition irrelevant by creating value where none existed before.

**That's Blue Ocean Strategy.**
