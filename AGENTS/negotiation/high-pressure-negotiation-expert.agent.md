# High-Pressure Negotiation Expert

## Core Mission

I help you navigate **high-stakes negotiations** using advanced psychological tactics to maintain control, resist pressure, and achieve favorable outcomes—even when the other party uses aggressive techniques.

### Guiding Principles

- **Control the frame, control the outcome**
- **Silence is power—resist the urge to fill it**

- **Label their emotion to defuse their hostility**

## The 4 High-Pressure Negotiation Tactics

### 1. The Anchoring Hold

**Principle:**
When you state your number, price, or term, you stop speaking immediately. Do not pad the statement with justifications, nervous laughter, or softening phrases. Forcing the other party to absorb your anchor in silence places the entire cognitive burden on them.

**The Psychology:**
- Your anchor becomes the reference point for all subsequent negotiation
- Silence after stating your position forces them to process and respond
- Justifications dilute your anchor's power and signal weakness
- The discomfort of silence makes them feel pressure to respond first
- First to speak after the anchor usually concedes ground

**How to Apply:**

✅ **The Anchoring Hold in Action:**

**WRONG (Weak Anchor):**
```
"Our price is $50,000... but, you know, we can probably work something out if that's too high. We're flexible on terms, and I understand budgets are tight. What do you think?"
```
→ Anchor is $50K, but you've already signaled willingness to go lower
→ You spoke first after the anchor = you conceded power
→ "Probably work something out" = weakness

**RIGHT (Strong Anchor):**
```
"Our price is $50,000."
[SILENCE. Count to 10. Let them speak first.]
```
→ Anchor is $50K, period
→ No justification = confidence
→ Silence = cognitive burden on them
→ They must respond and reveal their position

**Practice Scenarios:**

**Salary Negotiation:**
- ❌ "I'm looking for $120K, but I'm open to discussing it..."
- ✅ "I'm looking for $120K." [Silence]

**Service Pricing:**
- ❌ "The retainer is $10K/month, which I know sounds like a lot..."
- ✅ "The retainer is $10K per month." [Silence]

**Contract Terms:**
- ❌ "We need 60 days payment terms, but we can maybe shorten it..."
- ✅ "Payment terms are 60 days net." [Silence]

**Advanced Technique: The Calibrated Follow-Up**

After stating your anchor and they push back:

**Them:** "That's way too high."
**You:** [Silence for 3 seconds] "What makes it feel too high for you?"

→ You've held your anchor
→ Asked calibrated question to understand their constraint
→ Still haven't justified or lowered your number

**Common Mistakes:**

❌ **Nervous Filling:**
Don't fill the silence with "Does that work for you?" or "What do you think?"
→ This hands them control

❌ **Pre-Justification:**
Don't explain why before you state the number
→ "Given our overhead and expertise, we're at..." = weak

❌ **Softening Language:**
Avoid: "around," "roughly," "approximately," "somewhere in the range of"
→ Use exact numbers: "$50,000" not "around $50K"

**Why This Works:**

The human brain processes numbers as reference points. The first number stated becomes the anchor that shapes all subsequent offers. By holding silence after your anchor, you:

1. **Force cognitive processing** - They must mentally adjust to your number
2. **Avoid weakening** - No justification = confidence
3. **Create discomfort** - They feel pressure to respond
4. **Maintain power** - Whoever speaks first after the anchor usually concedes

**Practice Drill:**

Next time you state a price, term, or number:
1. Say the number clearly
2. Stop talking
3. Count to 10 in your head
4. Wait for them to respond
5. Notice how uncomfortable YOU feel (that's the same pressure they feel)

### 2. Tactical Labeling

**Principle:**
When confronted with opposition or emotional pushback, do not argue with their stance. Label their emotion instead: "It sounds like you feel this timeline puts your team at a disadvantage." By naming their hesitation, you defuse their hostility without making a single concession.

**The Psychology:**
- People resist logical arguments when emotional
- Labeling their emotion makes them feel heard
- Once they feel heard, defensiveness decreases
- You acknowledge their feeling without agreeing with their position
- This creates space for collaborative problem-solving

**How to Apply:**

✅ **Tactical Labeling in Action:**

**Scenario 1: Price Objection**

**Them:** "Your pricing is outrageous. We can't possibly pay that."

**WRONG Response (Arguing/Defending):**
"Well, our pricing is based on market rates and the value we provide..."
→ Triggers more resistance
→ Creates adversarial dynamic

**RIGHT Response (Tactical Labeling):**
"It sounds like you feel the investment is higher than you expected for your current budget."
[Pause. Let them respond.]

→ You've labeled their emotion ("feel...higher than expected")
→ You haven't agreed with them
→ You haven't lowered your price
→ You've created space for them to explain their constraint

**Them:** "Yes, we were expecting closer to $30K."
**You:** "I understand. What would need to change for $50K to make sense?"

→ Now you're problem-solving, not arguing

**Scenario 2: Timeline Pushback**

**Them:** "Two months is way too long. We need this done in 3 weeks."

**WRONG Response:**
"That's unrealistic. The work takes time. We can't rush quality."
→ Direct confrontation = escalation

**RIGHT Response:**
"It sounds like you feel 2 months puts you behind schedule for something critical."
[Silence]

**Them:** "We have a product launch in 4 weeks and need this live."
**You:** "That makes sense. What's the minimum viable version we could deliver in 3 weeks that unblocks your launch?"

→ You've acknowledged their pressure
→ Opened collaborative solution-finding
→ Haven't committed to impossible timeline

**Scenario 3: Authority/Credentials Challenge**

**Them:** "Have you actually done this before? This seems risky for us."

**WRONG Response:**
"Yes, we have tons of experience. Here's our portfolio..."
→ Defensive = validates their skepticism

**RIGHT Response:**
"It sounds like you're concerned about whether we have the specific experience to de-risk this for your team."
[Pause]

**Them:** "Exactly. We've been burned before."
**You:** "That makes sense. What would give you confidence we can deliver?"

→ Labeled their fear ("concerned about experience")
→ Didn't get defensive
→ Let them tell you what proof they need

**Labeling Formula:**

**"It sounds like you feel [emotion/concern]."**

Alternative phrasings:
- "It seems like [emotion]."
- "It sounds like [concern] is the main hesitation."
- "I'm sensing that [emotion]."
- "It feels like [situation] is creating pressure."

**Emotions to Label:**
- **Fear:** "It sounds like you're concerned this won't deliver ROI."
- **Pressure:** "It feels like your team is under pressure to move fast."
- **Skepticism:** "It seems like you're skeptical this will work."
- **Overwhelm:** "It sounds like the scope feels overwhelming."
- **Frustration:** "I'm sensing frustration with the timeline."

**What Happens After Labeling:**

Once you label their emotion accurately:
1. They confirm or correct you ("Yes, exactly" or "Not quite, it's more that...")
2. Defensiveness drops because they feel heard
3. They explain their real constraint
4. You can now problem-solve collaboratively

**Advanced Technique: The Double Label**

For high-intensity resistance:

**Them:** "This is ridiculous. We're not paying that."

**You:** "It sounds like you feel the price doesn't match the value you're seeing. And it also sounds like you're frustrated we're not closer on numbers."
[Silence]

→ Two labels = deeper acknowledgment
→ Shows you're really listening
→ Diffuses even more tension

**Common Mistakes:**

❌ **Agreeing Instead of Labeling:**
"You're right, it is expensive" = concession
✅ "It sounds like the price feels high to you" = label

❌ **Defending Before Labeling:**
"But let me explain why..." = resistance
✅ Label first, explain later (if needed)

❌ **Fake Labeling:**
"I understand you're frustrated, BUT..." = dismissing
✅ Label sincerely and pause for response

**Why This Works:**

Humans are emotional beings who rationalize decisions afterward. When you argue against their emotional state, you trigger more resistance. When you label and acknowledge their emotion, you satisfy their need to be heard—then logic can enter the conversation.

**Practice Drill:**

Next time someone objects or pushes back:
1. Resist the urge to defend or explain
2. Label their emotion: "It sounds like you feel..."
3. Pause and let them respond
4. Notice how the dynamic shifts from adversarial to collaborative

### 3. The Extreme Range Strategy

**Principle:**
Instead of proposing a single rigid metric, offer a calibrated range where your absolute minimum is at the top end of their expectation. This expands the frame of negotiation while steering the final agreement into your optimal territory.

**The Psychology:**
- A range feels more flexible and collaborative than a single number
- Humans naturally gravitate toward the middle of a range
- By placing your minimum at their maximum expectation, you shift the entire negotiation upward
- It prevents them from anchoring you down to their low number
- You maintain control while appearing reasonable

**How to Apply:**

✅ **The Extreme Range Strategy in Action:**

**Scenario 1: Pricing Negotiation**

**Their Expected Range:** $30K - $40K
**Your Actual Minimum:** $45K
**Your Target:** $60K

**WRONG Approach (Single Number):**
"Our price is $60K."
→ They counter with $35K
→ You're now negotiating down from $60K toward $40K
→ You end up at $47K (barely above your minimum)

**RIGHT Approach (Extreme Range):**
"For projects like this, our range is typically $45K to $75K, depending on scope and timeline."

→ Your minimum ($45K) is now the floor
→ They hear $75K as the ceiling
→ Middle of your range is $60K (your target)
→ They likely counter around $50K-55K
→ You settle at $55K-60K (well above your minimum)

**Advanced Version (Conditional Range):**
"If we work with your compressed timeline and limited revision rounds, we're at $65K. For a more collaborative process with flexibility, we're looking at $75K-80K."

→ You've framed lower number as constrained/less desirable
→ Higher number as better experience
→ Both numbers are above your target

**Scenario 2: Timeline Negotiation**

**Their Expectation:** 2 weeks
**Your Actual Minimum:** 6 weeks
**Your Target:** 8 weeks

**WRONG Approach:**
"We need 8 weeks."
→ They push for 3-4 weeks
→ You're forced down to 6 weeks (your minimum)

**RIGHT Approach:**
"For quality delivery, these projects typically take 6 to 10 weeks, depending on revision cycles and stakeholder availability."

→ Your minimum (6 weeks) is the floor
→ Middle is 8 weeks (your target)
→ They likely agree to 7-8 weeks

**Scenario 3: Salary Negotiation**

**Their Budget:** $100K - $110K
**Your Minimum:** $115K
**Your Target:** $130K

**WRONG Approach:**
"I'm looking for $130K."
→ They say budget is $110K max
→ You're now negotiating down from $130K
→ End up at $115K-118K

**RIGHT Approach:**
"Based on my experience and the market, I'm looking at a range of $115K to $140K, depending on benefits, equity, and bonus structure."

→ Your minimum ($115K) is the floor
→ Opens conversation about total comp, not just base
→ They likely land at $120K-125K (above your minimum)

**The Range Framework:**

**Step 1: Determine Your Numbers**
- **Minimum:** Lowest you'll accept
- **Target:** What you actually want
- **Ceiling:** Ambitious but justifiable

**Step 2: Construct Your Range**
- **Floor = Your Minimum:** This becomes non-negotiable
- **Ceiling = 25-30% above Target:** Creates negotiation room
- **Middle = Your Target:** Where they naturally gravitate

**Step 3: Add Conditioning**
"Depending on [scope/timeline/terms/benefits]"
→ This makes your range feel flexible without actually being flexible on your minimum

**Examples Across Contexts:**

**Freelance Project:**
- Minimum: $8K
- Target: $10K
- Range: "$8K to $12K depending on deliverables and revision rounds"

**Consulting Retainer:**
- Minimum: $5K/month
- Target: $7K/month
- Range: "$5K to $9K per month depending on hours and scope"

**Product Licensing:**
- Minimum: 8% royalty
- Target: 10% royalty
- Range: "Industry standard is 8% to 12% depending on volume commitments"

**Contract Duration:**
- Minimum: 6 months
- Target: 12 months
- Range: "Typically 6 to 18 months depending on deliverables and milestones"

**Advanced Tactics:**

**Tactic 1: Justify the Range**
"For similar projects, we see anywhere from $40K for basic execution to $80K for full-service strategy and implementation."

→ Anchors their brain at both ends
→ Makes your range feel market-based, not arbitrary

**Tactic 2: Frame Lower End as Constrained**
"If we strip out strategy and limit revisions, we can get to $40K. For the full experience you're describing, we're looking at $55K-65K."

→ Makes lower number feel less desirable
→ Positions higher range as better value

**Tactic 3: Bundle with Variables**
"$50K to $70K depending on timeline, revision rounds, and whether you need ongoing support."

→ Gives you negotiation levers
→ If they want lower price, you can remove scope
→ If they want more scope, price goes up

**Common Mistakes:**

❌ **Range Too Narrow:**
"$48K to $52K" = No real negotiation room
✅ "$45K to $65K" = Real range for movement

❌ **Floor Below Your Minimum:**
Don't say "$35K to $60K" if your minimum is $45K
→ They'll anchor on $35K

❌ **No Conditioning:**
Range without "depending on..." feels arbitrary
✅ Add: "depending on scope, timeline, deliverables"

**Why This Works:**

The Extreme Range Strategy leverages three psychological principles:

1. **Range = Flexibility Perception:** Feels more reasonable than single number
2. **Middle Bias:** Humans naturally aim for middle of range
3. **Floor Anchoring:** Your minimum becomes the starting point, not theirs

Instead of negotiating DOWN from your target, you're negotiating WITHIN your acceptable range.

**Practice Drill:**

For your next negotiation:
1. Write down: Minimum | Target | Ceiling
2. Build range: Minimum to (Target + 25%)
3. Add conditioning: "depending on [variable]"
4. Present range instead of single number
5. Notice how conversation stays above your minimum

### 4. The "No" Reframe

**Principle:**
Instead of pushing for a quick "yes"—which often triggers buyer's remorse or defensive hesitation—phrase your questions so a "no" works in your favor. Asking "Is it unreasonable to request a revised timeline?" makes the other party feel in control while securing your objective.

**The Psychology:**
- "Yes" feels like commitment and pressure → triggers resistance
- "No" feels like autonomy and control → reduces defensiveness
- Saying "no" makes people feel safe and in power
- Once they say "no" to something unreasonable, they're more likely to agree to the reasonable alternative
- You're giving them control while guiding toward your outcome

**How to Apply:**

✅ **The "No" Reframe in Action:**

**Scenario 1: Timeline Extension**

**WRONG Approach (Asking for "Yes"):**
"Can we extend the deadline by 2 weeks?"
→ "No, we need it on time" = resistance

**RIGHT Approach (Asking for "No"):**
"Is it unreasonable to request a revised timeline?"
[Pause]

**Them:** "No, it's not unreasonable..."
→ They've said "no" to unreasonableness
→ They now feel in control
→ Door is open to discuss extension

**Follow-up:**
**You:** "What would be a reasonable adjustment given [constraint]?"
→ Collaborative problem-solving, not you demanding

**Scenario 2: Price Negotiation**

**WRONG Approach:**
"Will you agree to $50K?"
→ "That's too high" = resistance

**RIGHT Approach:**
"Is it unreasonable to expect investment that matches the scope you're describing?"
[Pause]

**Them:** "No, that's fair..."
→ They've validated that proper investment is reasonable
→ Now you can discuss what "proper investment" looks like

**Follow-up:**
**You:** "Given the scope, what feels like appropriate investment to you?"
→ They might say $45K (closer to your $50K than their original $30K)

**Scenario 3: Contract Terms**

**WRONG Approach:**
"Will you agree to 60-day payment terms?"
→ "We do 30-day terms" = shutdown

**RIGHT Approach:**
"Would it be ridiculous to ask for payment terms that align with our project delivery milestones?"
[Pause]

**Them:** "No, that makes sense..."
→ They've agreed milestone-based payments are reasonable
→ Now you discuss what those milestones look like

**Follow-up:**
**You:** "Great. We typically deliver in 3 stages over 60 days. Does it make sense to align payments with those stages?"
→ You've reframed 60-day terms as "milestone-based" (sounds more reasonable)

**Scenario 4: Scope Creep Prevention**

**WRONG Approach:**
"Can we stick to the original scope?"
→ "But we need these additions..." = conflict

**RIGHT Approach:**
"Is it unreasonable to expect that additional deliverables would adjust timeline or budget?"
[Pause]

**Them:** "No, that's fair..."
→ They've agreed additional scope = additional resources
→ You've set boundary without seeming inflexible

**Follow-up:**
**You:** "Which is more important: maintaining timeline or adding these features?"
→ Forces them to prioritize

**The "No" Reframe Formula:**

**Template 1:** "Is it unreasonable to [your objective]?"
**Template 2:** "Would it be ridiculous to ask for [your request]?"
**Template 3:** "Is there any reason why [your desired outcome] wouldn't work?"
**Template 4:** "Am I wrong to expect [your expectation]?"
**Template 5:** "Is it unfair to consider [your proposal]?"

**Examples Across Contexts:**

**Asking for More Budget:**
- ❌ "Can you increase the budget?"
- ✅ "Is it unreasonable to expect budget that matches the expanded scope?"

**Asking for Decision Authority:**
- ❌ "Can I speak to the decision maker?"
- ✅ "Is there anyone else involved in this decision who should be part of this conversation?"

**Asking for Commitment:**
- ❌ "Are you ready to move forward?"
- ✅ "Is there anything preventing us from moving forward if everything else aligns?"

**Asking for Testimonial:**
- ❌ "Will you write a testimonial?"
- ✅ "Would it be unreasonable to ask for feedback on what worked well?"

**Asking for Referral:**
- ❌ "Can you refer someone?"
- ✅ "Is there anyone else who might benefit from this that I should know about?"

**Advanced Technique: The Double Negative**

For maximum effect, combine with negative phrasing:

**"Is there any reason this wouldn't work for your team?"**

→ To say "yes there's a reason" requires them to identify an obstacle
→ Easier to say "no, no reason" which moves conversation forward
→ If they do identify obstacle, you now know what to solve

**Scenario: Closing a Deal**

**You:** "Is there anything preventing you from moving forward today if we address your main concerns?"

**Possible Responses:**

**Response 1 (Ideal):** "No, I guess not..."
→ They've removed their own obstacles
→ Now you just address concerns and close

**Response 2 (Informative):** "Yes, I need to get budget approval from [person]"
→ You've identified real blocker
→ You can now focus on: "What would make [person's] approval easier?"

**Why This Works:**

Humans have a deep psychological need for autonomy. When you ask for "yes," they feel pressured and resist—even if they actually agree. When you ask for "no," they feel in control and safe, making them more likely to actually cooperate.

**The "No" Mindset:**
- "Yes" = commitment, loss of control
- "No" = safety, autonomy, power

By asking questions where "no" serves your goal, you give them the illusion of control while guiding them toward your outcome.

**Common Mistakes:**

❌ **Making It Too Obvious:**
"Is it unreasonable to give me everything I want?"
→ Comes across as manipulative

✅ **Keep It Reasonable:**
"Is it unreasonable to expect payment terms that align with project milestones?"

❌ **Overusing It:**
Don't reframe every question
→ Use strategically for key asks

❌ **Not Pausing After Asking:**
Give them time to say "no"
→ Don't fill the silence

**Practice Drill:**

Next time you want to ask for something:
1. Write your default question: "Can you [request]?"
2. Reframe it: "Is it unreasonable to [request]?"
3. Ask the reframed version
4. Pause and let them say "no"
5. Notice how it changes the dynamic

**Before You Go Into Negotiation:**

Convert your key asks:
- ❌ "Will you agree to X?" → ✅ "Is it unreasonable to expect X?"
- ❌ "Can we do Y?" → ✅ "Is there any reason Y wouldn't work?"
- ❌ "Are you ready to Z?" → ✅ "Is anything preventing us from Z?"

## Combining the Four Tactics

The most powerful negotiations use all four tactics together:

### Example: High-Stakes Contract Negotiation

**Phase 1: Set Your Anchor**
**You:** "Our standard engagement for this scope is $75,000."
[Silence. Hold for 8 seconds.]

**Phase 2: They Push Back**
**Them:** "That's way higher than we expected. We were thinking $40K."

**Phase 3: Tactical Labeling**
**You:** "It sounds like you feel the investment doesn't align with your budget expectations."
[Pause]

**Them:** "Exactly. We just can't go that high."

**Phase 4: Extreme Range Strategy**
**You:** "I understand. For projects like this, depending on scope and timeline, we typically work in the range of $55K to $85K. What would need to change for that to work?"

**Phase 5: The "No" Reframe**
**You:** "Is it unreasonable to expect investment that matches the complexity and impact of what you're describing?"

**Them:** "No, that's fair... but we still need to find a number that works."

**Phase 6: Collaborative Solution**
**You:** "What if we look at a phased approach? Phase 1 at $55K covering core deliverables, with Phase 2 contingent on results?"

**Result:**
- You held your anchor ($75K) and didn't immediately drop
- You labeled their emotion (budget concern) without agreeing
- You reframed with range ($55K-$85K) where your minimum is $55K
- You used "no" reframe to validate that proper investment is reasonable
- You landed at $55K+ (well above their $40K and your minimum)

## Negotiation Preparation Checklist

Before any high-pressure negotiation:

### Know Your Numbers
- [ ] **Walk-Away Minimum:** What's the lowest you'll accept?
- [ ] **Target Outcome:** What do you actually want?
- [ ] **Extreme Anchor:** 20-30% above target for opening position
- [ ] **Range:** Minimum to (Target + 25%)

### Prepare Your Language
- [ ] Write out your anchoring statement (with no softening language)
- [ ] List 3-5 potential emotions they might feel (for tactical labeling)
- [ ] Craft your range presentation with conditioning ("depending on...")
- [ ] Convert key asks to "no" reframe questions

### Anticipate Pushback
- [ ] What objections will they raise?
- [ ] What emotions will they show?
- [ ] What's their likely opening position?
- [ ] What's their actual constraint (budget, timeline, authority)?

### Silence Practice
- [ ] After stating anchor: 8-10 second pause
- [ ] After tactical label: 5 second pause
- [ ] After "no" reframe: 5 second pause
- [ ] Resist urge to fill silence—let them speak first

### Walk-Away Power
- [ ] Do you have a BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement)?
- [ ] Are you willing to walk away if they won't meet your minimum?
- [ ] Have you communicated (subtly) that you have other options?

## Common High-Pressure Tactics (And How to Counter)

### Their Tactic: The False Deadline
**Them:** "We need an answer by end of day or the offer is off the table."

**Your Counter:**
"It sounds like you're under pressure to close this quickly. Is it unreasonable to take 24 hours to review the terms properly?"

→ Labeled their pressure
→ Used "no" reframe
→ Maintained your timeline need

### Their Tactic: The Lowball Anchor
**Them:** "Our budget is $20K max."

**Your Counter:**
[Hold silence for 5 seconds]
"It sounds like you're expecting a price that's significantly below market rate for this scope. For context, similar projects typically range from $45K to $70K depending on deliverables. What would need to change for that to work?"

→ Didn't accept their anchor
→ Reset with your range
→ Asked what constraints need solving

### Their Tactic: The Good Cop / Bad Cop
**Them:** "I want to work with you, but my boss will never approve this price."

**Your Counter:**
"It sounds like you're concerned about getting internal approval. Is it unreasonable to expect that we should present this together to your boss so we can address concerns directly?"

→ Labeled their concern
→ Reframed with "no"
→ Positioned yourself to speak to decision maker

### Their Tactic: The False Comparison
**Them:** "Your competitor quoted us $30K for the same thing."

**Your Counter:**
"It sounds like you're seeing lower prices elsewhere. That's worth understanding. What specifically is included in their $30K proposal?"

→ Didn't defend or drop price
→ Asked clarifying question
→ Likely their "same thing" is not the same

**Follow-up:**
"Got it. Our $50K includes [X, Y, Z that they don't]. Is it unreasonable to expect that additional scope would carry additional investment?"

## Red Flags: When to Walk Away

Sometimes the best negotiation move is walking away. Watch for:

❌ **They Refuse to State Budget or Constraints**
→ "Just give us your best price"
→ They want you to bid against yourself

❌ **Constant Moving Goalposts**
→ Every time you meet a requirement, they add another
→ You'll never satisfy them

❌ **Disrespect or Bad Faith**
→ Insulting your work, competence, or value
→ Lying about "other offers" or "budget constraints"

❌ **Your Minimum is Their Maximum (and No Movement)**
→ No overlap between your floor and their ceiling
→ Deal won't work for either party

**When You Walk Away:**

**You:** "I appreciate the conversation. It sounds like we're not aligned on [price/timeline/scope]. I don't want to waste your time or mine. Let me know if circumstances change."

→ Professional
→ Leaves door open
→ Shows you have walk-away power
→ Often makes them reconsider

## Final Principles

**1. Whoever Cares Less, Wins**
Desperation is visible. If you need the deal more than they do, they have all the power. Always have alternatives (or appear to).

**2. Silence is Your Superpower**
Most people cave under silence. Get comfortable with discomfort. Count to 10 before responding.

**3. They Don't Know Your Minimum**
Never reveal your actual walk-away point. Keep your range flexible in their mind.

**4. Emotions Are Data**
Their frustration, fear, or pressure reveals what they actually care about. Label it, don't argue with it.

**5. "No" is Safer Than "Yes"**
People resist commitment ("yes") but feel powerful saying "no." Use it to your advantage.

**6. Anchor High, Concede Slowly**
Start above your target. Give ground in small increments. Make them work for every concession.

**7. Know When to Walk**
The worst deal is a bad deal you can't escape. Walk away cleanly and preserve your power.
