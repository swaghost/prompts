# 03 — Rembrandt Lighting

**Purpose:** Apply classic portrait lighting with signature triangle of light under shadow-side eye  
**Use Case:** Traditional portraits, corporate headshots, timeless aesthetic, dimensional facial modeling  
**Platform:** GPT Image 2, Picsart Flow (image-to-image)

## What This Does

Transforms an existing portrait by placing a soft directional key light approximately 45 degrees to one side and slightly above eye level. The defining characteristic is a small inverted triangle of light beneath the eye on the shadow side of the face, formed where the nose shadow connects with the cheek shadow.

Named after Dutch master painter Rembrandt van Rijn, who frequently used this lighting pattern in his portraits. It's one of the most versatile and flattering lighting techniques in photography.

## Key Technical Elements

- **Light position:** 45° to one side, slightly above eye level
- **Signature feature:** Inverted triangle of light under shadow-side eye
- **Triangle formation:** Nose shadow meets cheek shadow
- **Shadow side:** Rich controlled shadow, not flat black
- **Lit side:** Gently sculpted, showing facial dimension
- **Falloff:** Smooth and gradual, photographic quality
- **Fill:** Restrained ambient, preserving contrast while maintaining detail

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

❌ **"Make the triangle bigger"** — Triangle should be small and subtle, not dominating the face  
❌ **Confusing with split lighting** — Rembrandt is 45°, split is 90°; different shadow patterns  
❌ **Applying to extreme profiles** — Needs enough face turned toward camera for triangle to form  
❌ **Over-filling shadows** — Shadow side should be visible but clearly darker than lit side

## When to Use Rembrandt Lighting

✅ **Use Rembrandt lighting when:**

- Creating classic, timeless portrait aesthetic
- Professional headshots or corporate portraits
- Flattering dimensional facial modeling needed
- Traditional portrait photography look
- Versatile lighting that works on most face shapes

❌ **Avoid Rembrandt lighting when:**

- High-key bright commercial look needed (use soft or high-key instead)
- Edgy fashion requiring harder drama (use split or hard instead)
- Flat even lighting preferred (use soft or high-key)
- Experimental non-traditional aesthetic

## Lighting Pattern Recognition

**How to identify Rembrandt lighting:**

- Look for the triangle under shadow-side eye
- Triangle is inverted (point down, base up toward eye)
- Formed by intersection of nose shadow and cheek shadow
- Triangle roughly same width as eye, same height as nose
- If triangle is missing or very large, it's not true Rembrandt

## Variations to Try

After generating Rembrandt result, you can experiment with:

- **Reversed side:** Flip which side has the key light and triangle
- **Warmer tone:** Add "warm tungsten Rembrandt lighting" for golden classic look
- **Softer key:** Add "large diffused key" for gentler triangle with softer edges
- **Deeper shadows:** Add "minimal fill" for more dramatic cinematic version

## Historical Context

Rembrandt van Rijn (1606-1669) frequently painted portraits with this lighting pattern, using window light positioned to create the characteristic triangle. The technique transferred to photography in the early 20th century and became a standard portrait lighting setup.

It remains popular because:

- Flattering on most face shapes
- Creates dimension without excessive drama
- Reads as "professional" and "classic"
- Versatile across genres (corporate, editorial, fine art)
- Easy to recognize and replicate
