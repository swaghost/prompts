# 06 — Hard Lighting

**Purpose:** Apply focused directional light with crisp defined shadows and strong contrast  
**Use Case:** Fashion editorial, masculine portraits, sculptural emphasis, dramatic edge definition  
**Platform:** GPT Image 2, Picsart Flow (image-to-image)

## What This Does

Transforms an existing portrait by using a small, focused directional light source positioned to one side and slightly above the subject. This creates crisp, clearly defined shadows with minimal transition between illuminated and shadowed areas, emphasizing the natural structure of the face including cheekbones, nose, jawline, and other dimensional features.

Hard lighting is the opposite of soft lighting — where soft uses large diffused sources with gradual transitions, hard uses small focused sources with sharp shadow edges.

## Full Relighting Prompt

```
Relight the uploaded image using authentic photographic hard lighting while preserving the original subject, facial identity, expression, pose, hairstyle, clothing, jewelry, composition, camera angle, background, and image proportions.

Use a small, focused directional light source positioned to one side and slightly above the subject. Create crisp, clearly defined shadows with minimal transition between illuminated and shadowed areas. Emphasize the natural structure of the face, including the cheekbones, nose, jawline, lips, neck, shoulders, and collarbones.

Keep the shadow direction physically consistent with the light position. Preserve realistic skin texture, pores, fabric detail, and controlled highlights without smoothing or softening the image. Use strong contrast while retaining enough information in the shadows to keep the subject dimensional and photographic.

Do not create artificial shadow shapes, digital masking, a flat spotlight effect, or random patches of darkness. Do not alter the subject's identity, anatomy, pose, wardrobe, background, framing, or perspective. No visible light source, excessive glow, haze, glossy artificial skin, beauty retouching, or fantasy effects. The final result should look like the original photograph was professionally captured with a small, direct hard-light source.
```

## Key Technical Elements

- **Light source size:** Small and focused (bare bulb, small reflector, direct sun)
- **Shadow edges:** Crisp and clearly defined (hard transitions)
- **Shadow placement:** Directional and consistent with light position
- **Contrast:** Strong, high-contrast look
- **Texture:** Preserved and emphasized (skin pores, fabric weave visible)
- **Fill:** Minimal, allowing shadows to stay deep
- **Highlights:** Controlled but potentially specular on skin

## What Gets Preserved

✓ Subject identity and facial features  
✓ Expression and emotional quality

✓ Hairstyle and hair texture  
✓ Clothing, jewelry, accessories  
✓ Camera angle and perspective  
✓ Composition and framing  
✓ Background and environment

## What Changes

- Shadow edges become crisp and defined
- Transition between light/shadow sharpens
- Facial structure becomes more sculptural
- Contrast increases dramatically
- Texture becomes more pronounced
- Overall mood becomes edgier, more dramatic

## Hard vs. Soft Light Physics

**Hard light characteristics:**

- Small light source relative to subject
- Direct, unmodified beam
- Crisp shadow edges
- High contrast
- Texture-revealing
- Examples: direct sun, bare bulb, small beauty dish

**Soft light characteristics:**

- Large light source relative to subject
- Diffused, scattered beam
- Gradual shadow transitions
- Lower contrast
- Texture-softening
- Examples: overcast sky, large softbox, umbrella

Understanding this distinction helps choose the right technique.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

❌ **"Make it softer"** — Hard lighting is intentionally crisp; softening it makes it not hard lighting  
❌ **Applying to beauty portraits** — Hard light emphasizes texture and flaws; usually unflattering for beauty work  
❌ **Confusing with split lighting** — Split is about direction (90°), hard is about quality (crisp edges); can be combined  
❌ **Adding glow or haze** — Hard light is clean and direct, not atmospheric

## When to Use Hard Lighting

✅ **Use hard lighting when:**

- Fashion editorial with edgy aesthetic
- Masculine or character-driven portraits
- Emphasizing facial structure and bone definition
- Sculptural, dramatic, high-contrast look desired
- Revealing texture is important (architecture, products)

❌ **Avoid hard lighting when:**

- Flattering beauty portrait needed (use soft instead)
- Client has texture concerns (skin texture, wrinkles emphasized)
- Corporate/professional headshot (too dramatic/edgy)
- Natural soft documentary style preferred

## Lighting Position Variations

Hard light can be positioned anywhere; direction determines shadow pattern:

- **45° above and side:** Modified Rembrandt with hard edges
- **90° side:** Split lighting with crisp division
- **Directly above:** Downward nose shadow, eye socket shadow
- **Low and side:** Upward dramatic shadow with crisp edges
- **Frontal:** Minimal shadow, emphasizes texture

The "hard" quality stays constant; position changes the shadow shape.

## Variations to Try

After generating hard-lit result, you can experiment with:

- **Warmer tone:** Add "warm hard tungsten lighting" for golden dramatic look
- **Cooler tone:** Add "cool hard daylight" for cleaner modern aesthetic
- **Multiple hard sources:** Add "dual hard lights from both sides" for cross-lit sculptural effect
- **Harder edges:** Add "direct unmodified hard light" for maximum edge definition

Hard lighting carries specific genre/aesthetic associations:

Understanding these helps match lighting to project intent.
